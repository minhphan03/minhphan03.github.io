Again, spoiler alert.

Regarded as one of the best western movies of all time, "The good, the bad and the ugly" (Italian: "Il buono, il brutto, il cattivo") (I can’t stop the urge to add an Oxford comma right there) is a masterpiece of cinematography, music (the score is honestly impeccable), and acting. As with all the highly rated movies I’ve watched, I went in expecting a lot from this, and it did not leave me disappointed. My mind was totally blown. This shouldn’t be surprising, because I already had high hopes due to the fact that it is a western movie. I’ve only watched one other film belonging to this genre (or so I assume)—"Paris, Texas"—and it was also a masterpiece in terms of cinematography and meaning, so if there was something I hoped this movie would deliver, it was that.

This is my first movie of the Hollywood legend Clint Eastwood, and I was definitely not familiar with his game at all. Not only him, but all three main actors, including Lee Van Cleef and Eli Wallach, were charismatic, charming (in their own characters), and acted as if their rent were due. In this blog review, I don’t go into exalting and praising how perfect this movie is, because you should see it for yourself, and I am a firm pro-choice believer; instead, I wander into interpreting the movie as a whole, which, over the course of almost three hours of runtime and spending almost three days to finish, may have caused me to miss some of the key details. Again, this is a personal opinion piece and should be treated as such. I swear I didn’t reuse any of the r/TrueFilm submissions on the movie’s interpretation, just like the others I’ve done before, so take this one with a grain of salt.

Where should I start? Ok, the title: "The Good," "the Bad," and "the Ugly." With Blondie (Eastwood) as "the Good," I assumed in the most basic sense that he was the one with integrity and some discernible empathy among the three. We all saw him tending to a dying Confederate soldier as Tuco abandoned him to search for the gold himself. "The Bad," represented as Angel Eyes (Van Cleef), was sinister and embodied the greed and animalistic, inconsiderate nature that can be amplified in human beings. Merciless killings and torture to get what he wanted—Angel Eyes countered "the Good" by embodying the inherent truth that our nature is driven by competition and survival. $200,000 isn’t the price of one’s shedding his persona, but the price of amplifying it: all three characters wanted to get their hands on the hefty prize, and that shows greed; but only one would go about it in an inhuman way to speed-run his path to wealth. Tuco abandoned Blondie and blackmailed Bill Carson for information, but those moments didn’t define him. Angel Eyes, however, was sinister and cunning. He knew he couldn’t get a word out of Blondie, so he strung him along with six of his men, ready to finish him off as they unlocked the treasure. He managed to get information from Tuco, too. It’s animalistic greed that drives his actions, and in this western world where (toxic) masculinity prevails and drives the community of the new frontier, surviving means obtaining power, and obtaining power means growing wealth. New opportunities for men to explore the American Wild West open up struggles to stay afloat in this showcase of the violent, animalistic, inherently raw nature of humans, where set beliefs and established social and ethical structures are trampled and torn away gorily. Angel Eyes, as maleficent as he was, didn’t disappoint us in his betrayal of these characteristics. He wasn’t afraid to infiltrate the Union army, fishing for information on the treasure, stringing its captain along with empty promises that his troops would soon see victory, and abusing Bill Carson’s girlfriend to find his whereabouts.

But these actions were stealthy, executed in secretive, private settings. Angel Eyes was "the Bad," but he wasn’t *the Ugly.* His actions were beautifully crafted in the craftiness of a well-done mask. This brings me to the meaning of *the Ugly* in the role of Tuco. For me, at first, it was difficult to define what makes someone *the Ugly* right after I finished the movie. Is it “ugly” because Tuco represents all of us—the ugly ubiquity of humanity and our intrinsic desire to be excluded from the very definition of the common man? Is it because Tuco can never be as graceful and all-mighty as Blondie or as cunning as Angel Eyes? Then it clicked. I speculate that "the Ugly" refers to a materialistic yet obtuse person—a man who only sees what’s in front of his eyes; someone who only thinks in the short term. We see this throughout the movie: Tuco was too trusting, believing Blondie when he said the treasure lay beneath the grave of a man named Arch Stanton. As soon as he saw the gold, the notion that Blondie had a gun and he didn’t completely slipped his mind. He abandoned his course of action to save Blondie when the latter admitted he had been given the details of the cemetery. Given how western movies portray the nature of men, this is certainly the case here. It’s the ugliness of a materialistic person who only thinks of his own fortune despite everything else going on in the setting of the movie: dying men, poverty, war. That’s probably the reason why there is not only the “good” and the “bad,” but also a third element, more ethically grey and worth dissecting. The character Tuco hence added a worthy depth into the movie, further expanded the concept of survival and human mannerism in the well established western genre.

Maybe I read too much into this, but isn’t it exciting how there is so much packed into this seemingly simple film? A typical film about discovering a treasure doesn’t have to be this deep, but it is. Maybe it has something to do with the spaghetti western subgenre, of which this movie is a staple? I wouldn’t have paid it any mind if not for the fact that the movie wasn’t filmed in the U.S. at all. The original title is in Italian, and most of the filming and production took place in Spain, not the United States. I was surprised to find this out, as I’d never imagine there’d be anywhere in Italy vast and barren enough to film those sandy dunes, wooden saloons, and ruined villages. Just like a U.S. historical drama, it references major U.S. historical events like the Union vs. Confederate war, and I think it’s so cool that Sergio Leone, an Italian filmmaker, could portray the ongoing U.S. geopolitics and capture the essential western style so well. I haven’t watched much of the genre, but there’s certainly a notable shift here: a subtle turn toward a more worldly lens in the genre itself, and less emphasis on action. Can’t wait to watch the rest of the trilogy!
