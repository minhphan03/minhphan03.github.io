I recently got tested for HIV. The result was negative, so that was a huge weight lifted. I didn’t remember the exact reason why I was so confident I wouldn’t get HIV and naively believed what my sexual partners disclosed about their sexual statuses, but for a few months before this, I was. I started going on Grindr, then Sniffies (both are popular homosexual hookup applications) and back to Grindr again, meeting strangers to satiate my sexual needs. I used condoms most of the time, but curiosity about barebacking clouded my judgment. I got tested once three months ago and again recently, using OraQuick—an HIV test kit that uses oral fluid and HIV antibodies to detect the presence of the virus.

Since it looks for antibodies, which are only produced as the body’s immune system fights off the pathogen, the kit’s window period (the time between initial contact with the virus and when the test can reliably detect its presence) is significantly longer than more modern testing methods—90 days—compared to RNA testing (which looks for the virus’s genetic material in a blood sample), which has a window period of around 14 days, or the more affordable and widely available p24 antigen/antibody test (p24 is a protein found in the virus itself and is typically tested alongside antibodies). I had no clue about this window period until I felt sick two weeks after a risky encounter when, if I remember correctly, I had unprotected sex with a guy I met on Sniffies. I don’t remember if I asked him whether he was clean, but we did it anyway.

I came down with a fever, a terrible sore throat, and chills four weeks later. They could have been symptoms of a typical flu or COVID-19, but my overzealous and overthinking brain wouldn’t let go of the possibility of HIV. I somehow associated these symptoms with HIV because that’s exactly what the overthinking Minh would do. To be fair, the typical acute phase symptoms of HIV are the same as the flu’s—our bodies naturally respond to viruses in similar ways. The virus is sneaky because you can have zero symptoms and still carry it. I read people’s stories about HIV testing and their experiences living with it across the internet, and of course, it didn’t help at all. I grew more unsettled. My brain went haywire, and I couldn’t focus on anything at work. I operated on autopilot while my heart was on fire. I just kept inwardly spiraling into the worst-case scenario—how I would live, how I would tell my parents if I contracted the virus. I grew scared.

As a twenty-one-year-old sexually active guy, I’m probably the last person who wants to seal my fate with this virus. As much as I understand that HIV patients can live long, fulfilling lives with antiretroviral drugs (ART), the stigma surrounding the virus is still pretty universal—especially in Vietnam, where my parents are still quite conservative. These treatments in the U.S. aren’t cheap either, even with insurance. Despite already having done the OraQuick oral test a week ago, the stories I read about its unreliability shattered me. I thought my $50 investment would give me peace of mind for a while, but in the end, it only gave me more doubts. I didn’t meet that many guys, but if the window period is that long, what if I actually had contact with an HIV-positive partner in the past few months and unknowingly carried the virus—and possibly infected others too?

In a moment of panic, I scrambled to schedule an HIV blood test. I’ve never hesitated less about spending money. A blood test with a shorter window period would cover most of my potential exposures. For the first time in years, I really prayed. I prayed and prayed for it not to be HIV because I could literally take cancer over it—at least I wouldn’t have to explain to my parents how cancer happens. It just does. If I had HIV, they’d be able to connect the dots right away. Homosexuality is still taboo to them. Even if they’ve come to terms with it, an HIV diagnosis would be a death blow to their acceptance. I wasn’t ready to find out, and it was relieving to be reassured that it wasn’t going to happen.

To this day, there are still over 30,000 new cases of HIV every year in the U.S., according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), with over 37,000 diagnosed cases in 2022 and 39,000 in 2023 (an increase!) across all U.S. territories. Newly infected cases in 2022 were estimated to be around 31,000. Across the globe, in 2024 approximately more than 40 million people are living with the virus, and with 103 countries and territories out of over 200 reported their testing numbers of only over 200 million who tested in the same year, the hard truth is, we still have not yet fully grasped how big the number can be. You see, it’s important to note that these numbers only reflect reported cases, which means undiagnosed patients are not included—and until they get tested, serious risks continue to loom around us. Some people only bother to get HIV diagnoses years after acquisition because the virus doesn’t have distinct symptoms, and may not leave any signs for up to a decade. Children who are born with the fateful illness, or a quick, fleeting unprotected encounter with an untreated, unaware person can leave stains they never have a clue about until it's too late. Isn’t that scary?

Over forty years since the height of the HIV/AIDS pandemic, we still haven’t found a cure. Despite the good news that more and more people can live long and healthy lives with the virus (less than a million died compared to the 40 million who was living with it), no one can lie that a one-time treatment is always preferable than taking pills for the rest of their lives. There are talks of an FDA-approved vaccine (lenacapavir) that one must receive twice a year for full effectiveness, but until it becomes affordable—and anti-vaxxers change their minds—a large population will still have to live with it indefinitely. A slip of carelessness, a mistake made in the heat of the moment, can cost you your confidence, your fortune, and your social life forever. There's a big chance it happens—unless you manage to embrace it (and not everyone can). Shame is a cruel thing. As treatment toward the LGBT+ community becomes more hostile, vulnerable individuals are alienated and more likely to take risks in exchange for freedom of expression. Treatments like PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) using Truvada and Descovy as oral or Apretude as injectable options according to the CDC., despite being shown to be highly effective in prevention, are factually still expensive, so uninsured or low-income populations have less access. I’m part of this community—and for a long time, I just trusted what my partners said about their statuses, without considering that some of them might be lying to get into my pants. It doesn’t help that stigma is still widespread, even though more people are educated on the matter. People living in shame may refuse to disclose their statuses and unknowingly spread the virus to others in their communities—queer or not. That’s why HIV is still prevalent today, especially in minority communities and third world countries, where resources for patients are scarcer.

In hindsight, this experience was a wake-up call. If I hadn’t gotten sick and been deeply stressed about testing, I probably would have risked more exposure to the virus by now. I met a guy last weekend and insisted on using a condom, even though he had tested negative for most STIs. I mean, it’s better to be protected than not, and who knows who else he met in the weeks between his test and me? With that being said, I think I should tone down my sexual rendezvous. I know how risky and superficial they seem, but damn—if I didn’t get a huge ego boost from all that attention (!!). You can only be young and sexually active for so long before age catches up to you and you become a sexual veteran at the age of 30 in the gay community.
