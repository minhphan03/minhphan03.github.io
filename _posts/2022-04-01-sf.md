People visited San Francisco could not help but being amazed by its cultural richness and marvelous sights: Golden Gate Bridge, Chinatown, the endless skyscrapers, people from all corners of the city. I was too, at first: how couldn’t I? It was San Francisco after all, one of the most iconic cities in the world.

I perused San Francisco through walks and buses — while walking gave me an authentic feeling of what the city looks like in its glory, buses filled the empty holes in which dark corners bared themselves to our naked eyes, ones its people never bothered to hide, and visitors never bothered to find out themselves.

I saw how glorious and tempting the city is under the sun: beachey fairs, casual pedestrians strolling and laughing with their friends, busy shops yelling out orders, and seagulls swiftly flying overhead. They radiated youthfulness – the kind of energy that you rarely see in any other city in the US. Maybe it is why people love it so much. It offers so much opportunities to thrive and succeed.

People came here for various reasons: to engage in a diverse, open community that SF uniquely developed over its memorable history; to find their own opportunities in big cities (just like any other one across the globe); or, to stick to where their jobs are. They all contribute to an evergrowing economy, one that never seem to reach its apex.

To many, it has lost its spark long ago: exhaust fumes, traffic congestion, trash overflowing, homelessness, robbery, crime-ridden neighborhoods, unimaginable living costs, etc. you name it. All as results of San Francisco’s irresistable facade. People got hooked and moved to the city looking for reasons to stay, and some could not wait to leave the place behind. I understand. Most major cities suffer from them too. It’s only natural.

But a lot of people found what they need. They found tight-knit relationships and networks fulfilling their missing pieces. They found their dream jobs. They found the lifestyle they looked for. They found the reasons to stay. I understand if people constantly mock the city’s bad deals, because they got stuck in it unwillingly; but what’s life if you cannot observe it on a larger scale?

I have learned to be grateful to be able to gracefully overcome unfortunate events. I, myself, find my trip to the city to be unfortunate in many ways — missed flights, overpriced donuts, and even unresolved arguments with people — but after all, they were all valuable lessons that shaped up who I am today. I would not learn the costly way if it were not for them. Furthermore, I had many first-times during the trip that I forever treasure.

I roamed across Baker Beach at 11pm, listening to the waves crashing into the shores and the moonlight shone above my head. I sat on the rocks, reveling in the chilling winds and bopping my head along Charli XCX’s new album “CRASH”. I took pictures of the Golden Gate bridge while eating the donuts I bought in downtown. I rambled among seas of people at the San Francisco piers, eating hotdogs and sightseeing the culture. I visited an old record store, hearing people talking about nonsense. I talked to one of my roommates at the hostel.

I took so many pictures like I have never taken anything before, and I say this out loud because it was weird. I have been here before, around 5 years ago. But that San Francisco and the San Francisco I revisited were drastically unalike. Maybe I was getting older and came to enough terms with experiences throughout these years to not being able to look at the city the same anymore.

I never had to think about where to go, what to expect, incidents that could happen the first time I came here. As a little kid, I only had so far a single thought when my father took me for a walk at the Golden Gate Bridge: I visited a very cool place! Not that the Bridge became less cool after that, but now that I was getting older I got very introspective here and then, and could not help but thinking about how these tourist attractions have become somewhat less impressive and more ubiquitous.

Regardless of my worldview then, I enjoyed San Francisco very much. After all it was my first self-travelling trip ever, and it was truly a remark of my teenage year. Being a pseudo-introvert, I don’t fancy going out unless with friends, so you know it must be hell of an insane decision to embark on this trip. I broke all the rules I have set in stone for myself and decided to do something for a change, to renew my image and to break free from the jaded routine of school.

San Francisco may not be an ideal place to visit considering how populated and popular it is; however, I am confident enought to say out loud that I was able to create very special and unique memories without anyone else’s input. I went to places tourists rarely visit, to eat food tourists rarely eat, and live places tourists rarely stay. I reluctantly found the rare beauty among the commonplace mundaneness. But of course, with prices to pay.

When I first set my foot back in Seattle, my emotions were filled with exhaustion and regrets: I paid so much for an overpriced and completely off-the-plan excursion. However, now that I look back at it, I’m ready to let this negativity go. San Francisco with all of its problems is still beautiful in its own ways, and I’m truly grateful to be its visitor. I have grown so much from it, and what could be more beautiful than realizing that fact?
