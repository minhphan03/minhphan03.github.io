My first time watching a non-narrative (documentary/anthology?) on the big screen was a big success. I thought no one would arrive at the theater to watch it, but it turned out there was a decent audience for my showing. Along with the other two posts, these three make up a three-part series of the Palestinian genocide. To clarify, recently I was more reserved in speaking out about it on social media, partially because it would risk my student visa status (I'm studying in the US right now, and the fact that multiple international students had their visa terminated due to political involvement online put me in a position to prioritize my safety first). Which, and I clearly acknowledge this, leads to my gradual sense of dissociation and desensitization (I briefly covered this in my last part of the series). It's painful to witness a minority being wiped out in real time, and we couldn't do anything, as the majority of us today are too oppressed by our own systems that having the ability to reach out and support others unfortunately seems like a stretch. In times like these, we should educate ourselves about these matters, to reach for the universal truth and find compassion through art and journalistic media. This movie is one of them.

Unlike the previous two posts in the series on the Israel-Gaza conflict, in this blog post, I offer some thoughts through words on what some of the short films meant to me. To anyone who doesn't know, this is a collection of 22 submissions about lives in Gaza under the IDF attacks, and was selected as the Palestinian entry for the Best International Feature Film at the 97th Academy Awards, making the December shortlist. Spoiler alert has been warned — proceed with caution.

**Out Of Frame (Nidaa Abu Hasna)**

This one is heartbreaking. I'm sure all others are too, but still, it does not mean this one is not. A girl who made art and dreamed of showcasing her art to the whole world was crushed by the war, where the IDF had demolished most of her studio space. A future, a dream soul-crushingly demolished. A beautiful frame that was her bright possibilities was shifted away suddenly, out of focus and out of frame. Her sculpture, which was made of mosaics, personally conveyed a grand meaning of the beauty behind the ruins, that broken hearts and souls can still generate warmth and value if only we choose to embrace hope.

**Charm (Bashar Al-Balbeisi)**

This one is more abstract, almost fictional. If I remember correctly, it's also one of the shortest, with virtually no plot. Not really impressive in any kind of way, but the double exposure effects were cool. A super short short of a girl dancing with the ghosts of her missing friends, almost dreamlike and saddening. Innocent children are bearing the most trauma out of this war, and there is nothing we can do about it.

**Awakening (Mahdi Karirah)**

This short is the last one in the anthology, and was a recording of a puppet show of a father who lost his hearing a decade ago due to the bombing in Gaza, then regained it after another in 2024. Mahdi Karira, the director of the short, was a puppeteer. Before the war, he made marionette dolls and held puppet shows for children; however, his workshop was destroyed during the war in October 2023. The puppets in this short were built using empty cans, signifying a universal truth that art is deathless and can be found in the direst places. The Palestinian people were not unfamiliar with war (the father in the story lost his hearing a decade ago). Still, throughout time with new experiences, their confinement within the scope of evacuation and running away begged for a halt, a change, an awakening. His preference to lose his hearing again so that he couldn't hear any explosions and screams amplified that wish. They do not wish to perish, or to be flushed away to a foreign land: all they wish is a moment of peace and tranquility, something generations of innocent lives haven't had the privilege to enjoy.

**Jad And Natalie (Aws Al-Banna)**

A simple reminiscence from the narrator himself, "Jad and Natalie" were the names he and his wife had promised to name their children. However, the passing of his wife has left him broken and lost, as we can see him just roaming around the collapsed remains of his house, where they lived. We can see that the war spared no one, and even those alive (in this case the widows and widowers), a lot of the time, would feel no luckier or happier than the dead. Claiming Aws' wife's life also implies the demise and wipeout of a future generation through the demolition of families. What is the point of living if you have no one left to live for, and everything you built crumbles overnight?

**Everything Is Fine (Nidal Damo)**

Following a stand-up comedian as he prepared for a show later that night, "Everything is Fine" saw this person getting a haircut and showering in a made-up bathroom surrounded by UN-branded tents and smoke from the bombs. The most memorable part for me was when he heard the sound of a bomb while showering and had to evacuate to his tent, only to act like nothing had happened and dress up for the show afterward. In his suit, he walked through the ruined streets to the site to perform in front of the wounded audience. Like the title said, "everything is fine," he went on with his life and hobby of being a comedian, and despite going through the lows on his own, he knew that laughter is more needed than sorrow in these trying times, and swallowed his sorrow to put on a performance.

**Hell's Heaven (Kareem Satoum)**

The most outstanding and haunting short for me, "Hell's Heaven," showed a man waking up inside a dead body bag and recalled his day before on how he ended up there. Evacuated his home without any blanket to keep him warm through the night, he went to steal a body bag, knowing that if he died through the night, at least he would die peacefully in a warm shell. It's haunting because of the fact that he chose to get a body bag as if he knew death would seal his fate soon, and the body bag implies a resignation, a succumbence to death. Like "Awakening," "Hell's Heaven" depicts Gaza as a place where blood and dead bodies are drowning all willingness to live, and even the strongest ones can't fight against it. The fact that nobody prevented him from taking it was even more tragic because there were so many bags ready to be used, so nobody batted an eye; his death, if it eventually happened, would just be another number added to the dead count. What could be more depressing than that?

**No (Hana Awad)**

A woman attempting to find inspiration in music and positivity to cast away the painful affairs and finding her place within a group of young adults was refreshing to see after so many sob stories presented earlier. "No" to me is not a denial of the ongoing affairs, but a refusal to the negativity it brought and a declaration against what the world has painted Gaza to be: full of woes, casualties, and laments. To refuse to back down, keep your heads up, and stay positive amidst what's going on is not for the faint of heart, and I think it is beautiful that this short could point that out. We see the chants, the acoustic crew singing together, the lovely smiles and laughter the narrator managed to find and engage in. It's a reminder that no war can wipe out these courageous spirits, and when you have not much left to lose, what's quintessential is still your well-being because your community depends on you, and no one can tell you that you are trivial and unimportant.

**Taxi Waneesa (E'temad Weshah)**

Another outstanding story that showcased daily life in Gaza through the lens of a taxi driver, except the driver (Ahmad) rode a donkey named Waneesa instead of driving a cab. He served all free rides because he knew everyone was struggling to afford basic necessities. As Weshah, the director, came on the screen and announced his death, she mentioned how his martyrdom killed her drive to continue the film and affected Waneesa as she refused to leave him behind. This story highlights another aspect of the war: how it affects the nature and animals around them. Dying pets, pets with trauma, and service animals are silent sacrificers, and many of us take them for granted. The short's ending also left me with a haunting question: how many of these actors or those who appeared on this footage remain alive, if not wounded? 

**24 Hours (Alaa Damo)**

One surprise attack is traumatizing enough; how could someone even survive with three? Yet, when Alaa Damo's friend Mosab Al Nadi found himself under three airstrike attacks from the IDF, he was fortunate enough to be alive, buried under the ruins despite sheltering in one of the so-called safe zones in the area. Through these attacks, he lost all of his family members, and he never had a moment to grieve after one before another fell upon him. Those blurry captures upon his bloody face, limping and waiting for help, then circling his house where he built his family, then recalling how these attacks stole away the majority of his loved ones, broke my heart as if he tried his best to hold in his tears. His speaking about how he had his mother's face against him and discovering his father's leg was a moment I'd never forget. 

**Recycle (Rabab Khamees)**

One of the more memorable shorts in the collection for me is "Recycle." Following a daily recapture of a mother and how she incorporated and reused the scarce buckets of water allotted daily to do all the chores around the house, from initial clean water to rinse their food, cook, and drink, then reuse the water for bathing her children, for herself, then washing their clothes, flushing toilets, and watering their plants. The short did not just highlight the daily struggle of Gazans but also showcased their brilliant minds adapting to and making the best of the situation.

**Offerings (Mustafa Al-Nabih)**

Following a woman novelist's steps through the well-documented collapsed civilization, writer Diana El Shinawy talked about a long history of displacements. The reality settles in deeply when you hear it directly from somebody who was heavily impacted by it. She was alive to retell the stories of her ancestors, and like the question I asked after watching Taxi Waneesa, when the new generations of Palestinians wither away under the bombs and cruel massacres, will there be anyone who will continue to be the witness to all of these acts? The brutality of life was barely manageable for adults; would children survive, and for how long? The offerings that Palestinians had in the sacred, beautiful, culturally rich land now look nothing like what they once used to be. History is erased before our very naked eyes, and there is very little we can do about it. The long history of displacements was documented, too, but look at how forcefully it is disputed, refuted, and rewritten. What will the treatment be for the current history going on?

**School Day (Ahmed Al-Danf)**

This short moved me to the brink of tears. Children, with their innocence, how could their brittle hearts bear all of this suffering and loss? Following a short trip of a young child through the buildings' crumbles to the also non-existing school where his dead teacher could have been alive and continuing to teach him, and satisfy his hunger for knowledge. What could have been a typical school day turned to a repeated, endless reminder and mourning of lost ones, in those places where one may think that hell has taken over the drive for education and learning contrived to burn. For a child like him, typically, life is surrounded by schools and families, and just like how we often regard teachers as our second parents, it is totally touching to see how school becomes a safe place, even an escape from reality. To witness that dream die too, that is another level of misery. Our young boy, silent and determined yet equally lost, marched on to what used to be his school, but we all understood his feelings. If you have a heart, you would.

**Flash Back (Islam Al Zrieai)**

Another story about a child, this time a little girl, coping with her sonic trauma of artillery and planes by blocking out the world with her headphones. With those headphones, she got lost in her headspace and regained her childlike, juvenile behavior of her past (flashbacks) of drawing and dancing (her hobby), and enjoyed them for a little while. It saddened me so much to see how children have to deal with trauma, especially with those made-up or temporary solutions that you know probably won't last. When she put those headphones down and got pulled back to her present state, it was so crushing to imagine. However, they all deserved a break, a moment to escape that harsh environment and find their own will and power to continue dragging through the days. These children were born in the unstable affairs of Palestine, and they lived through worse conditions than both you and I ever did. If putting on a pair of headphones and practicing a hobby is what she could do to live like a child again, I'd say she totally deserves it. For tomorrow, she would never know what would happen, and if the bombs kept falling on her and her loved ones, at least she could be martyred, not in fear, but in grace, in doing what she enjoyed and never succumbing to cruelty. 

**No Signal (Muhammad Alshareef)**

A relatively simple plot of a man trying to find his brother under the rubble of a house that was destroyed by air missiles, what made "No Signal" so moving was the background noises of those missiles and the encounter with a man who was gathering wood for a fire. When everyone was doing their best to survive, gathering sticks around rubble was nothing short of abnormal. However, to the man himself, it was offensive, because how could a stranger understand what was keeping him on the edge, that it was his loved one who was under that rubble, and he had no idea whether he was alive or not. The brother picked up, but the signal died just a few moments later. He tried to console the daughter and promised to come back tomorrow to rescue as there was no gasoline available to power their only bulldozer. The uncertain ending gives us two questions: firstly, would the brother still be alive the next day, and secondly, how could they save him? In a way, I find this circumstance somewhat represents the current affairs of Gaza: their residents (the brother) were alive and under the rubble (the displacement/genocide), but outsiders (we civilians or people who condemn the genocide) could virtually do nothing to help (aid was not allowed to enter; resources were scarce). The person who gathered the sticks could represent people who were moving on in a world of their own and going on their business without a care, or those who benefited from the genocide. The phone signal itself was a call for help from Palestinians, or the whole documenting of the situation, and the fact that it ended abruptly may give a warning that time was running out.

**Soft Skin (Khamees Masharawi)**

This short begins with a stop-motion scene depicting a black bird (IDF) tearing up a vibrant and colorful community (Palestine), and the process behind it. Children in torn tents enjoy cutting out paper and sharing their stories about how the war has impacted their families. There were these children whose names were written on their bodies, and knowing the reason why broke my heart and haunted me. To have to face and acknowledge death at such a young age, and to feel the wrath of suffering, knowing that their futures were almost non-existent, was terrifying. A boy quoting, "I don't want them to write on my body," that line was powerful. I'm sure I think deeper than what he meant at the time, because its [the act of writing on the body] intention was depicted in the movie was because it helped these children not to face nightmares about the war. To me, it sounded like what he wanted was that it should never have happened to begin with. There should have been no bombing for body parts to be found and collected. And it was powerful and heartbreaking at the same time, because these children should never have been facing nightmares at all. They did not choose to be born and grow up in the war at all, so why do they have to prepare for death?

**Sorry Cinema (Ahmad Hassouna)**

Hassouna's dream of becoming a director was a love for fiction movies, yet he directed this short, describing the non-fictional struggles of a young adult growing up in the war. When life is tough, people tend to distract themselves with dreams and imaginary worlds, but what do they do when the world is too disruptive and it destroys their whole dream? He directed a beautiful fiction from his own dreams, but was not even able to watch and immerse himself in it on the big screen. Then ironically, he witnessed how his world blew up, with bombs that we all could only see in the fictional movies, with people picking up bags of flour mixed with sand—a blend of fiction and non-fiction that no one wants. People running for their lives, trying to survive... It's so real and disturbing for him. He gave up his dreams to focus on trying to survive in reality because dreams can't even give him a getaway anymore. I watched the mini-documentary, and it gave me such an epiphany: the reason why we enjoy war fiction so much on the screen is because we get the thrill of it that we don't have to worry about it in real life, but when it does happen in real life, suddenly it is not so thrilling anymore. Cinema here does not give him joy anymore; it reminds him of what he could not have — an escape to more beautiful worlds. He could not even leave Gaza now. So he said sorry to cinema, because he let it down and did not have the time and will to live up to pursuing it. But at the same time, this statement is cinematic itself. By giving up his dream and recording the non-fiction lives in Gaza for us to watch and witness on-screen, he did what cinema was meant to do: to unite, inspire, and empower us all.

**The Teacher (Tamer Nijim)**

Locking the lens on the crisis of basic human necessities in Gaza, this short documentary puts the audience through the life of a teacher who used to be in Gaza, who lost his job and had to survive on the humanitarian aid: water, bread, and power for his phone to connect with his surviving loved ones. When fresh water is scarce (as we already discovered in "Recycle" discussed above), those who were not quick enough to get it have nowhere else to go or ask for help, simply because everyone was suffering. He could not beg or cry or steal it from anyone else. He could only wait for another day, because your integrity is a part of who you are as a teacher. This short personifies a quiet, deeply moving perseverance resembling the spirit of all Palestinians.
