As a movie with a runtime of almost two and a half hours, I was not very sure that I would enjoy it. However, as I began to grasp the main themes and understand a little bit more about the characters, I now think this is a very interesting movie to write about. It hits a little bit too close to my heart as an international student who was forced to grow up too fast at the age of sixteen. I felt seen, I felt heard, and I felt all the emotions and frustrations that Akira, the big brother in the house, had to suppress as he turned a blind eye to please his mother and siblings, just like how I swallowed my pride to survive.

We saw glimpses of his childlike behavior: throwing a bottle, playing baseball with a stick, or reading comics. After all, he was only twelve at the time. But oh no, how rare it was to see these scenes.

Let's talk about the struggles. We were bombarded with the everyday struggles Akira had to go through, from finances (grocery, electrical bills) to simple conversations (with the stealing accusations from the supermarket owner, or the flashy friends at the arcade) and dreams (when he sighted the students at the arcade). They kept coming, incessantly. He could not confide in anyone, instead keeping his head low and determining his ways through each.

Growing pains, abandonment issues, childhood trauma, and getting stuck in a society that micromanages every single thread of your identity. None of them were allowed to go to school, the most basic thing a child should have. I saw Fukushima doing homework at the start and thought at least he got to go to school, but no, he had to pursue education on his own without any mentoring. Her second-oldest child, Kyoko, wandered around the house all day and was disallowed to go outside. She played with the small toy piano, dreaming of saving her own money to buy a real one. They were never promised a future to begin with, and from that moment, I realized that the movie was going to get even darker.

The mother hiding her children in the suitcases and lying that her husband was working abroad as an attempt to fit her image into the typical nuclear family ideals. Her avoiding telling her romantic interest about them. Akira lied to the landlord about his sister, saying that Yuki was just his cousin staying overnight. The pitied face that the owner gave him when he admitted that he did not have a father.

She was almost never at home, keeping them in the dark about her whereabouts, and solitude kicked in for the children. Then one day, she went for good. When Akira realized that, he did everything he could to reach her, but to no avail. That solitude grew heavier and tenser, but he had to keep up his facade and fake smiles.

"Am I not allowed to be happy?" - she had asked, exasperatedly.

Could we blame her? After all, just like them, she was also stuck in the disdainful stereotypes of a Japanese woman. She could not just let them go because they were children, and god knows how difficult it is for a woman with children to get into a relationship. It's understandable. Nevertheless, the fact still stood: she abandoned them. It was a tragedy in itself, a generational trauma that our society never attempts to address. She apparently needed help, but she was also to blame for all the things that happened to them. When I looked at her, I saw a helpless woman, one who loved her children so much but did not have the nerve to tell them the truth.

It is no help that the movie was filmed in blue tints and cold colors. Akira and his siblings often appeared in the frame mostly all alone, going through and through the same old places, like the same routine he ground through every day to keep his family at bay. When the lights were all yellow and bright, they were often nowhere to be found. We only saw them looking through the windows at these lights and people enjoying themselves, wishing they would be someone they were not. When they went outside for the first time in like forever, it was only for a bursting short time out of a childlike desperation, an effort to blend into this community they have been so deprived of. The kids were like fish in water, marveling at everything on the streets and even on the ground. But for the sake of their "images," when they came back home, they still had to hide in the closet, and nobody knew their mother had abandoned them.

We rarely saw him smiling. He did most of his chores in silence. Walking through the Tokyo streets, under the night lights, or even when he was with his siblings, no one can deny they could see his worries bubbling under the surface. Him thinking about their mom, him thinking about his future, him thinking about his siblings. When he spent his time with his very few (and bad!) friends, that was when he found his escapism. He let them overtake his duties in the family, deteriorate his traditional values, and he abandoned them just like his mother did. A gullible soul, too young to know what was right and what was not, we saw his struggles to fit into a society too nefarious and unkind. When reality kicked in and his mother stopped sending money, they did everything to scrape by: getting free food, cutting budgets, and dodging rental payments, selling clothes. The tipping point appeared when Shigeru chewed paper to forget his hunger. Akira's extended, bubbling frustration then drove him to destroy his toy car, and moments of pain followed. However, his self-pride prevented him from getting help from Saki, his "crush." As he and the siblings grew older, we saw how these growing pains impacted them: they stopped talking to each other, grew resentful, and isolated themselves from the world outside. Like the title of the film implied, "nobody knows" who they were. They were invisible in a world too busy to acknowledge their existence.

When Yuki became unconscious after a fall, his last bit of pride dissipated. He asked Saki for the money she gave him earlier to lay her body down under a vast field where airplanes flew above. The utter despair and wretchedness of having to bury your sister's body was enough of a traumatic experience for anybody, let alone someone who has gone through so much more like him. Worse, their mother was not even there, and even the burial was inside a suitcase, as if her life was reduced to nothing that not even a proper casket for her final days was affordable.

I watched this movie, haunted by the meaning and despair that the characters have gone through. It followed the exact definition of a sad movie through and through, from the sad beginning to the even sadder end. I likened the Fukushima family to the unfortunate families out there being hidden away for the sake of the glamorous city sights and riches. Nobody knows about them. They came and went. When one dies, nobody attempts to reach out. They became nobody, with no future and nothing to live for. All their attempts became futile as days went on, even when they reached out and were desperate for a helping hand. Without education, they could only go so far. Their future was doomed before they knew it. And the worst thing is, we don't know how it's going to happen next.
