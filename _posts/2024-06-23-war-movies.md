Personally, war-themed movies are either hits or misses. I know, because I watched a bunch of them. But for those that hits, they hits deeply. Losses, pains, bloods, tears, and the ephemeral existences of humans brought forth strong emotions everytime I watch them.  

**Mùi Cỏ Cháy (2012)**

Mùi Cỏ Cháy was a spectacular film, and I admit I am a little biased towards Vietnamese films as a Vietnamese myself, but it was one of the best war movies I’ve seen. I paid no mind to the special effects or how much budget that went into making these bombing scenes looking so real, because Mùi Cỏ Cháy was convincing enough to look like those scenes were deadly, suspending, and heartbreaking. I kept comparing it to Saving Private Ryan, one of the legacies of the legendary Steven Spielberg, but at the end it was baseless because while they both mentioned the themes of patriotism and lost of innocence, they were not that comparable. One was about WWII, one was about Vietnam War. One was almost three hours long and made me want to turn off half way through, and one kept me constantly engaged throughout a concise 92 minutes runtime. One depicted professional soldiers, the other found farmers and students turned amateur fighters. Hence, I found Mùi Cỏ Cháy was more touching: the main characters were never meant to be soldiers, unlike in the other movie where soldiers were already seasoned and prepped readily to fight in the battles (well, it doesn’t meant that they deserved to go on fighting in these bloodshed battles, but you get it). And that also explains why it resonated to me so much: I was barely 20, just like them, an age too young to understand what was going on in the cruel world but mature enough to know what’s to believe in. And it hurts looking at these young guys bonding over innocent, playful moments of youth having to witness each other perish away in the gruesome, inhumane torture they did not expect to find. I went in expecting that it would go all full-war, but it turned out that the pace was at a sweet spot, enough for me to savor this beautiful bond between the guys while hinting subtly of what’s to come.

The imagery in the movie was powerful. There was this white statue of a girl reading a book that the crew took together with before embarking into the bomb rains down south. Throughout the movie, as each of them fell down, the statue started to bleed, too. A statue, lifeless and inanimate, symbolic and innocent, now played a more impactful role in the movie: it represented the lost innocence of so many youths across the country, of dreams and promises that would never come true. Blood dropped on the book that she was reading symbolized a unremovable blot of history, an accusation of damage of the war on not just generations of Vietnamese people, but to everyone whose aspirations had to be deprioritized because of it. And no, this is not a propaganda for the Communist Party of Vietnam. This movie is just an adaptation of a war veteran’s diary, and how could one possibly cram propaganda into such a beautiful account without ruining everything about it? Focus on the priceless memories and friendships, as well as the traumatizing atrocities of these life-changing battles, every moment in this masterpiece rekindled in me a fond admiration and appreciation to the older Vietnamese generations whom had sacrificed their youth and themselves to give me and a hundred million other compatriots the peace we have today.

What made Mùi Cỏ Cháy so heartwarming and became an instant favorite of mine was that Nguyễn Hữu Mười decided to finish the movie on a hopeful note: captain Phong returned the blemish photo to Hoang after the Fall of Saigon in 1975. That photo went through the death of three of the members, but at the end of the tunnel at least someone was able to reclaim that lost memory and innocence. War will eventually be over, and the right side will prevail in the end. As long as there is belief in a righteous victory, everything will be possible. Maybe not now, not for a long time, but eventually. Millions of Vietnamese soldiers fell down in Quảng Trị, and to this day unexploded bombs were still hiding somewhere on the land, but this historic site finally belonged to Vietnam as it had been for thousands of years before these battles.

**Come and See (1985)**

Truly one of THE movies of the year. Haunting, disturbing, and heartbreaking, I am appalled at how much damage and deep an actual war movie can show on screen. Sometimes in the movie I kept wondering if these people were actually dying or getting shot, because those emotions and countenances were so raw and powerful. The narrator witnessed the most horrific facets of humans as they committed genocide and degraded humanity to shreds with wicked laughter and pleasure in hopelessness and despair. Villagers being shut inside concentration chambers before being lit up on fire and bombs; one girl being pulled around on her hair before gang-eaped by perverted Nazis; a body being paraded around like an exhibition for the masses; villages and houses being ruined; body parts in mud covered in flies. It’s raw and unimaginable to the point of nausea. Romanticization is a thing of the past: in Come and See, everything was stripped bare to showcase the most extreme trauma of humans. Lifeless eyes, bloody lips, deaf ears, bodies buried alive, a scarecrow made with a skull, constant screaming of innocents, chewing on grass. Never did a movie make me feel so uncomfortable, and man was it a terrible experience to have. I can’t fathom the degree of hatred that humans can possess to act on these most depraved desires and be proud of it. Or was it just brainwashing that makes the idea of genocide and ethnic cleansing become appealing and sound? If Mùi Cỏ Cháy depicted the loss of innocence through engagement in war, Come and See shattered it by the war crimes themselves: innocent victims lost their lives and beliefs as they got torn down by weapons, abomination, and depravity. Sadly, as much as these movies gave me a newfound appreciation on the human lives and the utmost devilry of how humans can be, watching them in the context of the ongoing world history also felt like an alarm: after so many years, we are still hopelessly witnessing innocent people perishing in spine-chilling, heartless war footages. It’s all the same, nothing has changed. In Palestine, Arabs got murdered by Israelis. In Congo, millions of residents got displaced due to cobalt mining violence and slavery. Papa New Guinea residents fought against the government of Indonesia to declare national independence. Unlike the Vietnam War which was able to move the hearts of millions of people, enough for them to develop a strong anti-war rhetoric in the US and pressured them into ceasefire, humanitarian aid is in embargo in Gaza. and the nobody bats an eye on the Congolese or Papuan crisis. Even terrible than that, most residents in Gaza were children and women, and they never had the ability to fight against the constant bombings that Israel was doing. Child labor in Congo was ubiquitous, risking and slowly killing the lives of millions of people just like a genocide in slow motion. The oppression in Palestine never started on October 7th, just like how the Vietnam War never started when the US first landed in the nation. Through indirect support and minor (and major) blowups, innocent lives were claimed just because they were born in the wrong era, in the wrong country, and were trying to survive through the day. This reminder was depressing. Two world wars later and we still haven’t found an appreciation in human lives. There are other countries besides these three whose residents are still living in fear and violence everyday, and now that I grown up and learned more about the world around me, the worse I found humans can be. Greed is bottomless, they say. It’s sickening.

However, if there’s any lesson I can carry from the movie to the situation in Gaza, Congo, and Papua New Guinea, it’s the optimism that one day bombs and gunfire will finally stop falling on these beautiful lands and the native people will live prosperously on the land without constant fear. It’s a cycle, simple as that. Karma always has the last laugh, and I don’t care that you think it’s impossible in this unjust world. You cannot turn a blind eye on these inhumane acts forever. You just can’t. If these people think that they can take over a country by using military tactics, committing genocide, and getting away with these acts, they are wrong, for they will learn the hard lesson of perpetual ridicule and isolation from the rest of the world. It’s already starting.
