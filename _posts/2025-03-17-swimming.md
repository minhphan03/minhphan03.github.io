*This piece has been sitting in my drafts for about a year. It was written when I visited India for the first time and was staying in my hotel room, just finishing a great book... For the anniversary of the trip, I decided to publish this. Words are unedited.*

I don’t know where to begin with this one. It may stay in the drafts forever or it may get published on my website. It may be 20 pages long, or it would got cut off abruptly with a simple explanation of “I don’t have the mood to write about all of this anymore.” It’s my stream of consciousness just spilling out these structures of words conveying a general idea of which I am not sure yet, but it’s steaming hot, rushed and endless as the days I’ve been dreaming of how I would do anything to be able to conjure up those lines as extravagant and layered as the authors of these books I had the pleasure to read and think about. 

Immense sadness. I guess this is the primary emotion that I am feeling right now. Immense might be a little bit exaggerated, but I do have an intense wave of sadness sweeping over my body, washing me off any exciting plans I have for this trip in India I am supposed to be enjoying right now. I am in the middle of reading “Swimming in the Dark” by Tomasz, and the most words I can describe about it is noncommittal and bittersweet. There’s this sense of insecurity and nostalgic in the words he’ve typed, about the life of a gay Polish man over the years, as a boy who was questioning his sexuality and actively going through the lost of innocence, and as a man whose beliefs and desire so conflicting they ended up marooned that shell of a boy to drown in sleepless nights and endless wonderings. I wish I have the words to better describe the emotions that this book has given me, for I am somewhat grateful to possess this sudden urge to type up this piece, though most of the time they (the pieces of sentences taped together) never managed to replicate an ounce of emotions that I’ve truly experienced, much less the immense sadness I’ve gone through while reading the book. Quite frustrating, but I’m trying my best. 

What I want to say about the book is just the what-ifs that keep spamming my head ever since I put it down. I haven’t finished it, for the thoughts went haywire and overloaded my consciousness for me to focus. I know right from the start when I picked up the book that it was not going to be a happy-ending one. I mean, not one with a boon title “Swimming in the Dark.” The love between Janusz and the protagonist was strong but veiled from the reality of the political scene in Poland under the communist regime at the time. The former was dead-set on the promises of the Party, while the latter’s firsthand experience with the its terrible mismanagements (corruption, cough-cough) and woeful lives of his loved ones as a consequence of its ruthless, out-of-touch leaders defeated his hope for the future. They split amicably, but the memories they created and the love they made could never go unnoticed. Who could, when the person whom you have desired back in your juvenile days and have reciprocated your advances with tenderness would one day be oceans away and living separate lives as if you have never shared so many intense secrets and a part of yourself? I guess it’s all about the what-ifs that could never give us an answer that made me sad, for I am a hopeless romantic (anyone can tell, honestly), and all I want when I pick up a queer book is that somehow everything will be fine. But that is not always the case, and a lot of the times you need to face the reality of life that you are either too much of a coward to face or too stony and brazen-faced to do anything about it. 

As a realist, I’d say no. Not everything will be ok at the end, and I’ve grounded myself enough to tersely admit it. Just like the people I’ve meet and wonder what could’ve happened if I were more confident and unlocked more memories with them. What if I overcame my pride and prejudice and talked to another person in this class. What if I stopped doubting myself and apply for this opportunity and it would somehow land me something I could have never imagined? All these what ifs I put in a box in the most obscure corner of my mind, for it’s a Pandora’s box waiting to spill all of my trauma and sadness that I am currently experiencing. Then I would have to clean them up again, hoping that even if they ever come back at that point it would be at a convenient time like this where I would have the opportunity to spend some energy writing up something else that is more powerful and logical. But I need to focus on the moment first, trying to analyze what sort of bursts of energy is keeping me alive and motivate me to feel so defeated. I suspect it’s more than just the book itself. At the end it’s only fiction. But you know what’s real? The lives of people out there who are battling with shame and unfairness to keep their dreams from dying. It certainly does not have to be a forbidden love, a fight for freedom, for human lives can exist in multitudes of combined emotions and challenged unique to each of eight billions of us. Some have more challenging obstacles than others. I have mine, though dramatically I think while mine is threatening to build up a perpetual saddens and depression which I for sure don’t want to talk about, in India where I am typing these words it seems like it unlocked this revelation that shook me to my core: how human lives can be so woeful. It’s one thing to read on the news and books, but it’s another thing to witness this poverty and endurance of perpetual poverty while sitting in a car so expensive while its driver was boasting about their privilege. 

It was yesterday (again, at the point of typing this). My friend told me many times that it was an Indian thing, that the caste system was in effect for many years and there was nothing to be done about it. Billions of Indians lived through it, and they will live through it for a long time to come. I was sitting in that car when I first actively acknowledged this complete contrast of lives here: my friend’s biggest concern was how her boyfriend could not marry her because they were not in the same circle, and god knows what another person out there could do to survive another day. I shouldn’t pity them, for I am not to underestimate this sense of community I was so grateful to aid me when I was struggling in a foreign land, but man it’s so hard not to when all you can think about is how drastic it is sitting inside a luxury car like that observing how people carried on with their lives. An old man sitting on the street, unfeigned by the blinding car headlights for he was used to that kind of treatment; an old woman, limping through the streets under the extreme heat, asking for money; a young healthy male housemaid, which my friend said was a typical culture in India, getting bossed around and living in substantially worse living arrangements (not too much as dumps), that was a direct consequence of the caste system bearing down on them from birth. It was a nasty truth they could never be able to change.

It didn’t fully register for me that these young guys had to resort to do these kind of jobs. To them, it would just be a job, like those who run tables and such, but for me it was shocking to see. At the hotel I’m staying the servants were all men. In the grand scheme of the gender equality movement it shouldn’t be matter if they were all women. They were apparently young, virile men who had to do these jobs. Again, I shouldn’t pity on them, but in the West you will never see something like this. Not even in Vietnam. In Vietnam, maybe they would run bike ride shares (Grab, Be) or just typical restaurants, not as maids. That they would possess to some degree of mobility and independence over their careers, if you think about it. These Indian men, they were uneducated, I would assume so, for their lack of English comprehension skills. I’d constantly feel bad every time I talked to them because I wonder what if they never had to live under the caste system and they actually had the opportunity to make a difference in their lives, like my friend’s best friend who were driving us in that luxury car. Who was, obviously, born with a silver spoon in his mouth. Instead of wandering around in the streets waiting for the day to be over, what if they managed to uplift the Indian society to overtake the reigns of the Western powers like the US, using the collective brainpower of a billion people? Because all I see was potential of a nation to rise if given the right treatment. My friend told me how the favoritism, political connections, and corruption was inherent to India, and you have to live and deal with it, and why does it sound so familiar here? It’s the same thing in Vietnam, where ignorance is bliss. One of the book’s main theme was about corruption, too. At this point of the essay I would say something promising along the lines of “but everything will be okay in the end” but damn in this perpetual immense sadness I have no hope for change. So maybe the best option is again “ignorance is bliss”? Just keep your head down and deal with it, like Janusz had told his lover every time he suggested running away? As long as you’re not being a hermit and actively working, just ignore any meta issue going on in the world? 

By no means do I want to steer this issue political. I can’t help, because any social issue is a direct consequence of politics. So here lies the ultimate question: what is the point of this essay, anyway? To inform the reader on how dire the situation is in India? To fawn over how engaging Tomasz Jedrowski’s first novel is? To think that three hours ago I’d have no idea this post would ever happened is to admit that perspicacious thoughts truly can sway people’s existence. I thought about how my friend corrected me to not refer to her “staff” as “servants” and how people like her had so much privilege in life its so understandable that she wanted to stay in that bubble forever with all her privileged, upper-class friends whose businesses and careers were red-carpeted and established using relationships and money, that nepotism was regarded as a goodwill, a joke, a ubiquity they are so dappled in that I feel so bizarre, jarring, yet deeply interested in experiencing. The protagonist in the book, Ludwik, witnessed this privilege from one of his acquaintances Hania whose father was a leader in the Socialist Party and it deeply upset him how controlling and selfish these people could get to benefit themselves — raising food prices, silencing protesters, limiting resouces — and the people in their circles. In India it may or may not be that terrible: after all, it’s not the 60s anymore after the war; however, the resemblance is there. I bet in Vietnam or in the US scenes like this are not hard to see if you get to blend in with the upper circles, but to see this so clearly in the open like this in India brings me such immense sadness. In a city of 12 million people, space is too limited for everyone to mind their business or suffer in silence. Is it their fault for being poor wandering aimlessly in the streets days after days, and that for many Indian students the only way to emancipate the caste system and improve their futures is like Ludwik who flew the hell out of his country and away from his beloved ones? To get loans and study abroad in other countries, hoping to find a future in foreign lands and risk bankruptcy if all went wrong?

I asked so many questions, yet failed to focus on any. The more I see the less I believe in the beauty of this world, unlike many whose trips are to sightsee the wonders and bright sides of cultures. I by no means should act ungrateful for this opportunity to have a taste of this privilege, such as how this museum we went to would open to serve just my friend and I because she (and her family) has the authority to do so, and during that visit she recited the fact that a group of 10 people at least was walking behind us. It was truly insane! I feel myself understanding Ludwik’s words so much more deeply as I visited the city and engaged in a circle so drastically different from my own. No wonder how so many people write about it, recently in Kevin Wilson’s “Nothing to See Here.” This immense sadness gets worse the more I think about it, as this reverie of living carefree in an all-accommodating life is soon to get to an end and I need to return to the States and readjust to my jaded broken life. But I guess it is meant to be, because I would feel so bad living in a life like my friend’s when all I would think about is how pitiful everyone is and how disgusting of me to abuse that type of privilege, all ethical and martyr. She kept asking me if I liked what I’ve seen so far in Chennai, and I said yes and acted all excited and grateful every single time. Truth is, I did for some parts. Would she understand how terrible I feel everytime her friends drove recklessly through the streets because she got so used to her life here that I’d take forever to fully gasp. I cannot blame her for things she cannot control, and neither can her parents nor her friends. They were born in a society already so messed up (maybe they would not believe so) and rigidly fixed over thousands of years it’s nearly impossible to create a change. She admitted its faults, but reveled in it, simply because it protects her social status and sustains her family privileges for generations. She accepts the fact that she could only marry a man in her circle, that her current boyfriend was only short-term because his family was not to be married up, and arranged marriage is never out of the question. Oh, lives and disparity. I’m writing essays and diatribes just to say all the things people already know.
