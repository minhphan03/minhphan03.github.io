My first-ever trip to New York was not a smooth one, unfortunately. I was so close to giving it up and canceling the whole trip because I got stuck with flight problems for almost three days. I only released my (mental) breath after finally seated on the aircraft itself, which was eventually running on the departing lane. If you want a brief recap on what was happening at the time, you’re welcome to do so, but I believe that it’s always better to leave it all behind and bury the hatchet would considerably improve the tone of this story. 

My plane did not land in New York. In fact, it stopped at Boston at Logan International Airport. It was 5 am back then, and chilling winds were… chilling. I took the bus to Midtown in New York and took another subway train to the Bronx, where I would stay for ten days. It was ecstatic, you know, seeing it for the first time through naked eyes. I saw the skyline, the skyscrapers, and the sea of people walking on the busy streets because the city is always like that. People from all backgrounds did not care what they would deal with when they first settled down in the metropolis, all because they had dreams to reach and efforts to survive. It was so fascinating how, after all these years living by myself, I would still feel lost in this wayward estrangement of sorts, despite just a new adventure to escape the stale state of schooling for a few weeks. On the way back to the Bronx, so-called New Yorkers intrigued me: they carried vibes so different from where I lived. I could not pinpoint exactly what the differences were. Maybe they were more alienated, or the atmosphere was just gloomy all around despite almost the end of the year. Everyone was silent, but you can still hear some small talk here and there, or when someone shouts gibberish or harasses the state of affairs that no one would bother to report; apparently, it’s somewhat of a learned lesson, a mutual muted agreement that it’s not worth it to intercept and make a fuss. Overall, you can feel this somberness in the thick air, which the dull grey colors of the subway interiors do not help.

That, interestingly, was where I felt like New York was at its most diverse. It does not stop at just race and class because you can tell that there is a noticeable mixture of natives and travelers, of children and elders, of people in business and artists, of students and vendors, and all the types of people you can think would appear at the city. New York City is a melting pot that way. Unfortunately, the fusion ended there. Once you leave your stop and step up from the subway into the daylight, you can surely see how heavily zoning in NYC is, especially in the Bronx, where I live. Maybe I don’t have a rich understanding of the area’s history myself, but twenty minutes of walking through the area and you will see why. Fordham University, a private institution in the area, was surrounded by two distinct communities non-overlapping each other: the Little Italy with white people and the Hispanic-Black community on the other side with entirely different architectural housing styles and amenities. A couple of steps and you would enter a completely separate entity. Obviously, it happens in Seattle too, but it was subtle, I guess. I’m not talking about Chinatown in downtown Seattle versus the areas around it, like Pioneer Square or First Hill and Beacon Hill, because it is more or less an attraction for tourists. The zoning here is sharp and rough and borderline dizzying. Was it a bad thing? I don’t know. Maybe not. You don’t want gentrification to reach where you live because it drives prices up, and your authenticity hollows out. But at the same time, you don’t want people to stereotype your neighborhood as “dangerous” because not many white people stay there. One of my friends asked me if staying in the Bronx was safe because she heard the area was dangerous and brimmed with crimes, and I raised my brow when I listened to their assumption. I agree that the area is more dominant with minorities, but it should never be regarded as “dangerous” because of that. Compared to Manhattan, it may not be as flashy or marvelous but have you ever wondered Manhattan is a popular neighborhood because of its history and not because of how many white people live there (again, I have to admit that it’s expensive to live in the area, and unprivileged people cannot afford such a place). Redlining allows harmful stereotypes to thrive and affect many communities of minorities. Their reputation suffers. Outsiders seem to think that the farther you live away from Manhattan and Central Park, the more barbaric (quite strong use of the word here) and underprivileged you are. I used to hold the same regard, but please don’t blame it solely on me. I had never been to New York before this, and from what the media force-fed and painted, it seems like Manhattan is the only place where upper-class (white) New Yorkers live. Even other popular neighborhoods surrounding Manhattan, like Brooklyn or Williamsburg, got gentrified so badly that it drove house prices to astronomical levels. Gentrified parts of Harlem like Hamilton Heights are considered safe, but imagine if gentrification never existed in the first place: enough people would be able to buy homes to live in that they wouldn’t have to wander around the streets and be considered “risks” for tourists. 

I am sure this matter is not black and white: intertwined forces and factors play essential roles in driving crime rates up. However, look at the city’s crime rate map (which I attached in the link below): Manhattan’s crime rate may feel as bad as the Bronx, but why is it more advisable to tourists than the other? Yes, the demographic does come into play: the more white people there are, the safer the illusion seems to portray. The steep division between these areas exacerbates this illusion and deteriorates the stereotypes, enabling micro-aggressions to spread wildly. People can and want to live within their native communities with their like-minded neighbors, but it should not solely determine how one evaluates where they should buy their next house. Imagine if you want to buy a home in a non-gentrified neighborhood, but then people tell you not to due to some misjudged stereotypes: how depressing can it be? 

Let us return to the original topic: the subway is where diversity profoundly manifests. You may not see the top 1% there, but you can see the rest 99% who arrived in the area for opportunities to change their lives. The existence of exclusionary rezoning happens mainly by the gentrification of white people and policymakers to drive minorities out of what they considered ideal living areas. It is ridiculous how you can only catch a glimpse of this ‘fair’ treatment underground, where redlining can never reach, and borderlines between neighborhoods seem to blur away somewhat. However, it is up to one’s interpretation that MTA is also underfunded, which, may I speculate, has nothing to do with macroeconomics and everything to do with race. Lack of investment into an improvement of the notoriously outdated system is so on-brand for a city that glorifies such a capitalistic image. Since many people depend on it to operate their daily activities, shouldn’t we do something about it?
