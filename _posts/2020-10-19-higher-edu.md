*An essay assignment I wrote for my Winter 2021 English Composition II course at Highline College.*

Education has often been the key to individual success, and the resolution to progressive change in society. One must go through schooling to observe his being and see where one is standing and consequentially developing oneself as a whole. Unfortunately, not everyone can afford education, significantly higher education which determines one’s future. Being expensive to the level of irrationality not only bring wretchedness to whoever pursues it but also becomes an epitome of how greedy money can drive the future generations to the dead-end.

Its irrationality has resulted in billions of dollars of loans that students cannot pay, which may lead to well-being deterioration. According to an article on Center for American Progress, “About 43 million adult Americans—roughly one-sixth of the U.S. population older than age 18—currently carry a federal student loan and owe \$1.5 trillion in federal student loan debt” (Colleen et al.) When one goes to college, he has to sign up for loans to assist his education. With thousands of dollars every month for up to six years of college, the total amount of tuition one has to pay can go up from \$18,000 per year for public institutions to nearly \$50,000 for for-profit institutions, which doesn’t include interest money over time while repaying (Tuition costs of colleges and universities). This amount of money does not include other considerable fees like housing, taxes, school resources and food. The impact of these costs on student well-being is profound. According to a survey of Purdue University, Lumina Foundation and Gallup, debt-free graduate students were more likely to have 15% and 10% better in well-being financially and physically, respectively (Dugan and Stephanie). As more students graduates, the job market becomes more competitive, with indebted students are more likely under pressure of finding jobs to repay their hefty loans, resulting in their mental health might likely to worsen. It is ideal to go to graduate schools to improve their chances, but look at the data: “Twenty-five per cent of grad students borrow almost \$100,000, and 10 per cent of grad students borrow more than \$150,000.” (Torkelson). Education became a nightmare for potential learners to further their studies because of its irrational cost, but it was not the only factor that ruins students’ capabilities. Depression, anxiety, and low life expectations are just a few of other issues, and college expenses all play huge parts in them.

But its toll does not end with students themselves. The irrationality of college expense also creates pressure on families whose children sign up for college. When we take into account of low-income families whose children aspire for life-changing careers the most, this is a lose-lose situation. Financial aid is guaranteed to cover higher education tuition for a long time after graduation, but what challenges students is that they eventually have to pay them off in full.  According to Sallie Mae’s 2020 report on how American families pay for their children’s college, an average of \$30,017 was to spend on each student, but not half of them come from their income (How America Pays For College). These numbers are alarming: the U.S. society has accepted the fact that most parents cannot cover their children’s college expense, and the number of loans they borrow will accrue into financial problems in the future. A lot of middle-class parents (which occupies more than one half of the U.S. population) prefer FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) other than student aids from private companies because of the former allows them to conceal their financial sacrifices from their children (Zaloom and Andrew). The fact that parents have to keep children away from their financial burden because they want them to enjoy college life is disturbing because that way of thinking will only lead them to money issues sooner or later. Furthermore, revealing the loan too late may also stress children to pay off the money to their parents, as it is only one of many coming of age issues that graduates have to face.

Finally, society is also affected on a wide scale if college tuition continues to increase over time. The effects are multifaceted: from discrimination to poverty and inequality. For one thing, a lot of people expect education to be the game-changer of their life. According to a report by Michael Hout, college graduates are expected to have better personal lives than high school graduates (Hout). They are also more likely to participate in social movements, like voting, civic engagement and social change (Hillygus). Increasing the tuition is threatening the learning opportunities of many students, and judging from a societal perspective, this also deepens the race and capital inequality.  The college net price accounted for over 25% of Black and Hispanic median income in 36 states, compared to only 16 for white families. (Mitchell). The lack of funding drives people-of-colour (POC) families to reluctance when it comes to investing in their children’s future. This is not a good sign, because this disparity in education affordance will only lead to other social issues as people resort to more cunning ways to deal with poverty and reduced access to education. A study conducted by Alma Gonzalez at New York University showed that “a 5% increase in college graduation rates correlates to a significant, 16.55% decrease in the homicide rate” (Gonzalez). Offering opportunities for students to participating in education, and getting degrees, directly reduces specific social issues, while also helps to obliterate racial prejudices and bigotries. The ever-rising cost of higher education, therefore, only hinders this long-term goal.

College students are not fully adults, and they are not financially independent enough to pay for their education without having debts weighing on them for the rest of their lives. They need open, accessible experiences to discover their future paths, not troubled minds drowning in the endless cycle of money and instability. Therefore, it is disappointing that the education system of one of the most advanced nations in the world cannot provide its citizens with an affordable approach to necessary sets of skills to keep its pace on the competition. Not only does the unaffordable tuition burdens students’ lives, but it distresses the whole society as well. We should act as fast as possible before the expenses spin out of control, especially when wealth distribution is increasingly disturbing and many families go down the poverty line, suppressing students’ diligence to change their lives.
