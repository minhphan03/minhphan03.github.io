*This is a true short narrative story that I wrote for my English Composition I class, 2020.*

It was a warm, delightful afternoon school break in the small school, with the mixed heatwaves of summer permeated throughout the blooming red phoenix flowers and warmed up the airy halls. Along with the heatwaves, as the soft sunbeam passed through through the trees’ evergreen leaves and darted the spotted sunlight onto the shadowed red brick-tiled yard, some young girls in their pink polka-dot dresses were chatting about their grades, boys in their blue uniform were chasing and yelling while playing hide-and-seek, and teachers were carefreely walking through the halls. In that seemingly leisured scene of the afternoon, Tuong, a close friend of mine, and I were assigned to clean the classroom. Right after the classmates got out of the room, we started lifting the wooden chairs onto the tables, rolling up our sleeves, picking up the old curled brooms and sweeping. We crawled to reach the dirty space underneath the rigged, bulky cupboards, picked up the crushed pieces of colorful chalks that former teachers accidentally broke when they wrote too passionately on the stained green board. Time passed by in ease. Tuong, after finished collecting the garbage, told me to fill up the water tank for cleaning.

When I came back with the filled tank and looked up at the clock, it struck me harshly that we only had roughly five minutes left to finish the chores. Immediately, I grabbed the mob, soaked it hurriedly into the tank and started to clean. Water streaks followed my careless mob rubs, but I didn’t bat an eyelid on them. Time was not to spend on cracking perfection. Being on the rush of time, I scrambled with the class board deck while Tuong was chilling with the chairs. I stepped backwards in line to my water streaks, bending my back to rub the cracking floor. “Minh, why would you rush so hard? You will never know what the consequences will be if you are reckless like that,” she confused, “Besides, they can wait outside”. When I told it was time was about to run out, she didn’t answer back, just carefully cleaning the chairs. No sooner had I continued bending my back forward and gliding the wet mob on the floor than my brain alarmed me that I just hit something right behind my back. The shattering glass sound followed. I was instantly out of my wits. I couldn’t help but standing still in such a helpless posture while my naive neurons tried to crack up the faintest idea of what happened. There was water leaking under my sandals, and I belatedly realized the class’ fish tank was broken.

“The fish tank is broken!,” a guy yelled, his voice twisted into the most violent, nefarious sound I had ever heard. Tuong looked up. Her eyes opened wide at me. “Minh! Do something!” But I couldn’t. What could I manage to do when something like this happened? I feared of being scold, of being punished in front of the class. My classmates surrounded me like I was a criminal ready to be convicted inside the court. What was the energy of carelessness now burdened by the powerlessness of falsity. I failed not to let the tears prevailed the trivial confidence that left in my vulnerable soul. My tears of shame and embarrassment was hot and cruel. It felt intermittently unstoppably on the powerless blush. 

I fumbled the dustpan and swept the broken glass away. Some of my friends helped to tidy up the “scene”. I was weeping intermittently, the numbnesss too preoccupied by the toxic thoughts to pick up anything. The apprehension was accumlating more and more as time passed by. The last two English periods of the day was nothing but me tritely waffling the overreacting seriousness of my wrongdoings to my friends. I only need somebody to warm me up and make me feel brave before the storm the following day. I hated it that there was only two negligible ways to mark the end of this: either their advice of me confronting my head teacher or my subjective but risky buying the replica of the fish bowl. I decided the latter. Somehow in my preposterous juvenility, living with the ego is in my wildest dreams, that I felt like living in my comfort zone was the best way to get rid of the gruesome truth out there in the world. I lied to my father that day that I was assigned to buy a fish bowl for the class. We searched up the whole town until the last vasts of afterglow dimmed behind the skyscrapers and the colorful led lights replaced them hurriedly as if the city would collapse behind my already wilting heart. As I saw the red headlights lashed pass my eyes, they also pierced through my faintest hopes, tempting to break it into million pieces. But they didn’t have the chance. The new fish bowl, valuable as unpolished diamonds and fragile like my vain hopes, was crookedly hiding in a dusty shelf in the back of an aquatic shop, behind the loosely hanged strings of mixed fish feed. The bowl was round, stylized with curved, petal-liked edges. 

But later my reason couldn’t ignore the cruel fate: I confided to him the backstory of it. Likewise, he told me to do the same thing as my friends advised, but I insisted on doing what I determined to do. In my dictionary years ago, there was no place for “hard-headed” to exist. But it didn’t last long: his righteous judgements talked me in consent for the call. He called her in place of me. Turned out the head teacher knew the incident after all! She told me to bring the new fish bowl in. Her voice was outright serious, that unlike the headlights, it was actual needles that fuelled with flames. I couldn’t sleep that night. I couldn’t quit the contigency of being scold in front of public, and it should have been my most embarrassed moment ever.

When I walked into the class with the new fish bowl the next day, everything was unusually normal: nobody mentioned the incident and cautioned me to the events ahead. But it didn’t make me feel more comfortable. I collapsed on the crooked wooden table, exhausted for what I had been dealing with. I set my heart into the tenuous glass of the bowl, held it tight like the flames were about to burn it alive. If only she ignored it and normalized everything again. But life gives the unexpected: no sooner had I placed it onto the floor than Ms. Nga walked into the class. I took a furtive look at her unforgettable appearance: her solemn attitude, unusual reticence and bold steps on the silent floor, trying to decode what she had in store for me that day.

When the class had settled, she suddenly called me up. I startled and my muscles, as hurtful as my pitiful conscience and sore as being contracted and fettered by the weightiest iron balls ever, signaled their determination to keep me from facing the final round. With those pains in mind, I tried to stood up and steered for her desk. The scene around shrinked into just me and her, and the fish bowl on my hand was my glasses for reality. Still I could sense all their eyes on me, as soundless as I could feel my heartbeat drumming loudly inside. When I came to her desk, I made so bold as to look straight into her eyes, struggled to hide my insecurity.

“Minh, you know what you did was wrong, right?” She said. I couldn’t answer back, as my mouth went dry and my voice lost in the moment. The only thing that I could do was to place the bowl on her desk, stand aside and constantly mumble the helpless sorry. “This is the matter of responsibility, and it would be way easier if you just committed your wrongdoings. I have never taught you to avoid responsibility, haven’t I?” She finally set the flames and burned my heart. My trivial bravery ran out too: I hung my head down, cried quietly. She sighed and told me to go back to my seat. 

As I went back, I still kept the posture. But at the same time, as the moment went over, the fetters was unlocked and gone…

A 5th-grade student is neither too young not to hold any responsibility, nor too old to acknowledge the matter of responsibility imposed on his shoulders right with a solemn attitude. When I was in 5th grade, the stance didn’t exclude me: I tried to manipulate my falsity and avoid responsibility when it locked me up in the cage of maturity. But in an ordinary day of April, a trivial fish bowl has changed my mind forever: it taught me a hard lesson about the role of responsibility that grows perpetually inside our minds.
