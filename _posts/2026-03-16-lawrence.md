This movie is a fun one, and one in a while that made me work my brain off after finishing it. It's one of the movies that must be watched on the big screen because a small laptop screen would never do it justice. Also, one that makes you read the reviews/analyses right after and urges you to write down some thoughts, which I am doing now. 

The rise and fall of a historical figure, which has been portrayed on screen so many times throughout film history, is at one of its most significant moments in Lawrence of Arabia. I myself acknowledge David Lean's capabilities, vision, and ambition to turn this into a four-hour epic. Still, I felt it may have gone on too long at some point. Not that I don't enjoy it at all, because I really fell hard for the marvelous cinematography and legendary score, one that surely would last for eternity thanks to the lively orchestral arrangements. These epics need orchestra as much as we all need water, because without a great grand score, a picture is nothing and would bore me to sleep. It gave me the same emotions as Gone with the Wind (1937), with both being awarded the Best Picture in their respective years. If Gone with the Wind entertains me with serious racism and poverty discourse, Lawrence of Arabia serves food for thought on individuality and imperialism.

Lawrence struggled with his identity when he first arrived in Arabia from Cairo. His desire was pure at first: he wanted to help the Arabs fight against the Ottoman Empire. However, as soon as his influence grew and he gained more authority over the battles and excursions, his identity changed: he was no longer an advisor on behalf of Britain; he was going against the British imperial machine. Finding out about the lies enraged him, and he took the matter into his hands to decide that Prince Faisal should not just go against the Ottomans, but also against the impending betrayal of the white men. Is it his fault that he took great concern over Prince Faisal's idea of self-governance of a territory, that these small tribes should come together and push back against the war machine? Of course, no, but he clearly crossed his boundary here. He had no authority to tell the tribes and their leaders what to do, which goes against his belief that they ought to decide on their fates. At one point, he joked and compared himself to the great Moses when talking about the remarkable journey across the Nefud Desert, and isn't it ironic? Of course, it's a joke, but it really illustrates how the concept of the white man's savior mentality is really conceptualized and naturalized among men, especially young men who were brought up in the cruel times of war and invasion. A brave man with the scorching hot individualism running through his veins, claiming how this risky, deadly journey was doable without necessarily considering the lives of fifty other men, was a little bit more or less narcissistic, but that's just me. Its success certainly fueled his ultimate desire to be a man of everything. 

He climbed to the top to see that the only way is down. His identity as a British man could never assimilate the Arab spirit, and he lost his battles, from the mental to the literal ones. His getting caught and assaulted by the Ottoman army was the first domino. It fundamentally altered his beliefs about what he was supposed to achieve after this mission. Was it the Arab independence that he became so desperate to be the one who granted it, or the respect of his fellow British men in the army that he once desired to fit in? He got promoted and finally earned respect, but in the end, he was still a puppet and in the dark about what's behind it all. Was it enough that he suffered through all of this, to watch the people he adored perish in the worst way, and in the end, betrayed by both the prince himself and the colonel who promised to grant self-governance to the tribes? What was it for? 

This identity crisis of the common man who mistook himself for someone greater was tragic, but not new. Think Jay Gatsby in "The Great Gatsby" (1925) or Charles Foster Kane in "Citizen Kane" (1941). These men were portrayed as being bigger than they thought they were. They were deluded into the thought that they could carry a seemingly glamorous reputation beyond everyone's wildest dreams, living the life of the powerful while yearning for even more. Still, the truth was that it was their downfall. Unlike the other two, Lawrence of Arabia describes a more introspective outlook into the ego of a fragile man whose idealism conflicted with his very own character. If Kane's demise was his greed and Gatsby's was love, Lawrence was his very own blood. As a British man, he found his utmost glory in the desert among the Arabs, unlike in the army, where he was just a misfit of a lieutenant; he took great responsibility and got through with the highest regard, but was it worth it to pursue even more? Greed is a human's basic instinct: we all want more. There's no blaming him if that's what Lawrence desperately tried to hold on to — because what else could he do? It's damed if he did and damned if he didn't. There was no way out for a man's ego once he got a taste of power. Eventually, he lasted long enough to realize that there was no winning, that power was just an illusion, and that perhaps his return to England was the best way out.
