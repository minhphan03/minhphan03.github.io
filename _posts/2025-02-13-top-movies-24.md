As much as I want, I think I’ve hit a movie fatigue this year. This is warranted, because looking back at the number of movies I’ve watched in 2023, the sole goal was to hit 365 movies and I still failed (yet if you consider TV show episodes as “mini movies” then the the final number would easily exceed that). Therefore, in 2024, I watched fewer new movies, yet found myself coming back to revisit more, either with those I’ve not watched for a while or those I did not initially like. For new ones, I’m happy to say that for those I’ve enjoyed, their quality is still top notch, and I’ve found myself writing more about them. I tent to keep them short, yet I still reserve some special posts for those I put in more than a few words. To me, a good movie does not have to be cinematically spectacular, but it has to have impact. Here are the twelve movies that left a big impact on me this year.

<h3>January: Requiem of a Dream (2000)</h3>

This Darren Aronofsky's masterpiece represented what I believed to be his own style (based on two movies of his I have watched, but also it's his most popular one on Letterboxd): quick-paced shots, hallucination, drug abuse, thrilling and somber cinematography effects, and what-the-fuck-i-am-watching vibes. Or whatever they all mean. I had the opportunity to watch *Pi (1999)* before this one, so I somewhat expected what I would see in this one: a total clusterfuck that left me on my edge and scramble to google and smash my keyboards looking up “what the hell does [all 1943947293858380 details] mean.” It went exactly like that, which did not disappoint me in the slightest. I love what it means to experience a good movie that left me wonder; an aftertaste that just refuse to leave for months, just because there are so many details that wowed me. The mother in also in hallucination and high on drugs most of the time, the son with heroin addiction and his girlfriend suffered from abuse... That last scene where his necrosis bruises showed, all nasty and queasy from heroin injection gone wrong... A movie for the history me thinks.

<h3>February: Perfect Days (2023)</h3>

This movie hits so close to home. As someone who is about to enter the workforce and had toyed with the idea of starting low, I would say that Hirayama’s figure gave me a lot of hope into my future. We all know that every job deserves its flowers as long as it brings good into the world, but in the hectic whirlwinds of life, we tend to be oblivious to the idea. Through delicate, caring moments that were all Japanese we saw him comfort Tomoyama, his frequent bar owner’s ex-husband, as he fought with cancer, give up space for Niko to sleep over, and lend Takashi money to make up with his girlfriend, all without expecting any kind of return, he was larger than life, achieving a holy status that we all too coward to sit back and realize that life is worth more than a competition to the top. In somewhat a repetitive plot, we discovered his mundanity in all its glory: to be mundane is to live a life that you are content every day. He lived in solitude, but he enjoyed the life he had: reading a book before bed, taking pictures from his film camera, frequenting his favorite restaurant, helping people out and seeing their smiles. Challenges spiced up his life: overcoming them made him feel powerful. When Keiko showed disapproval of him still working as a janitor, he cried inconsolably, but life went on and another day arrived and tears must come to dry. “Next time is next time. Now is now.” That was what he and Niko echoed back and forth for a few minutes long. It takes strength to truly live in the moment, to truly enjoy what one has and never expects anything else. Therefore, the title of the movie is very fitting: those perfect days could only exist in movies, because social pressures would never let us settle down with a job that Hirayama had: anyone would feel defeated, unmotivated, and helpless, just like Takeshi. He never had those perfect days, and neither did we.

<h3>March: CODA (2021)</h3>

Many people bashed *CODA* as they dubbed it as a Disney teen-romance movie when it was nothing but. The romance was fleeting, non-existent even. However, elements of friendship, acceptance, self-growth, and embracement were inspiring, potent, and emotional. What seemed like a simple plot was crafted beautifully, weaved with elements of deaf culture, fishing industry, and family values that I grew to relate and appreciate so much. The movie plot is simple, and in the current movie scene where sophisticated plots with intricate details and the undercurrent of meaning and themes has to overflow and fully soaked into every detail of what considered a “masterpiece,” I appreciated a well-done family movie for a change. *CODA* did it very well — it made the watching experience pleasant and easy, rare adjectives that I did not have the courtesy to encounter for a while.

<h3>April: No Country for Old Men (2007)</h3>

For April, I made an exception: I wrote its entry when the month was nowhere near its end, because I was confident that there was no other new movie that month could surpass *Monkey Man (2024)*, a crime-thriller action that infused cultural elements within the jumbled-up, crafty, dogged underground life underneath the rusted roofs and polluted streets. The Hinduism’s Shiva God’s interpretation of a symbol for revelry and revolution put the his symbolism of destruction into a new light and wooed me to a top-rated, five star with no hesitation. I thought their uphill battles and ill-fated dreams and how their vengeance against corruption would blow other movies out of the water, but it has to be the last day of the month when I found out the rightful winner of this month’s best new movie: *No Country for Old Men*. Also a crime movie, very thrilling, and shot with all Western influences over it, I just couldn’t put my phone down! Negative empty shots and isolated men in the desolated, barren, lifeless landscape, the hunt & chase between the two callous main characters (Moss & Chigurh) truly shined. In this light, men in their nature both repressed and exhausted their masculinity — a chuckle at a shot, a smirk at the muzzle, a groan at the wound, a blanching face at the danger. In *No Country for Old Men*, revenge, vanity, authority, greed, retrospection, and madness blended together at undefined contact points, perfectly coordinated to result in an exciting, meaningful, satisfying piece of cinema. How far do you go to claim a sum of cash? How do you deal with the nature around you: to contort and bend it to your will, or learn to adapt and be hyperaware of your surroundings? Truly, this is so refreshing of a movie given how few 5 stars I put out lately (I was so certain that it would be a while would I find my next favorite). It’s only April but I can tell this is one of my forever favorites already, judging just by how little I click into the screen to check how much time I have left to watch. I’m so easy to mentally check out, but for these masterpieces they deserve all of my attention.

<h3>May: The Pianist (2002)</h3>

*The Pianist* was rough to watch, just like most excellent war movies. It’s raw, unfiltered, and naked (though you can use these three terms interchangeably). If there is one thing I find it so hard to believe, it’s the violent scenes in these movies. How can you make rolling a tank over people and falling from the top of a six-story building so realistic or recapture the ruins and architectural details of these historical battles? You can argue that is the reason why the budget for these movies are so high, but for a $35 million budget, *The Pianist* is definitely an outlier to this story. Enough about cinematography — let’s talk about why this movie stands out among other Holocaust movies such as *Sophie’s Choice (1982)* or *The Sound of Music (1965)* or *The Zone of Interest (2023)* (well, the only three I’ve watched so far!). Again, the answer lies in its directness and rawness. You see, the topic does not hide behind a side storyline, and Polanski did not shy away from showing us the inhumanity that rotten minds can lead us to. It’s like an even better version of *Come and See (1985)* (of course with a different topic). To witness the main character Szpilman went through hope, betrayal, famine, imprisonment, isolation, separation from family, near-death situations was tragic, but as he was spared from death by a Nazi captain, that’s when I knew this movie would then be one of the best movies I’ve ever watched. Humanness always rise above devilry and hatred and discrimination and hatred, and survive eventually when it seems like everything is against them. Every inspiring survival story like that always showcases the raw capability of human beings transcending societies and culture differences. Whether it’s music or bravery or any other thing, the lesson remains: there is no limit to humanness.

<h3>June: It’s Such A Beautiful Day (2012)</h3>

If you happened to visit my Letterboxd, you would see that I did not give a rating to Don Hertzfeldt’s short animated movie. It was quite unconventional, and I needed more time to fully grasp the concept. The movie, for sure, was evocative. I was tempted to write a whole post discussing the multiple concepts that came across my mind during watching the movie, but with how many movie review posts are still pending, I better shorten this one. Concepts of perpetual reincarnation (looking at you, *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*), aging, genetic flaws, and depression made up a great deal in the movie, and you know how my overthinking self would waste no time steering away from the movie to get knee-deep in mulling over these. Coupling with the fact that my grandma recently lost her memory due to a stroke, the feebleness of human lives looks even more terrifying now. I’m only 20, an age many would guess too young to fret over deteriorating health and wasting time on earth, but thanks to the movie this existential crisis comes back in full force, doubling down on my current lack of directions and financial dependency. Full on recognition and verbalization of something I haven’t really thought about for a while. Bill going over his mother’s handwriting book that she practiced to tell him she loved him; her photo album of all the relatives he had never met and probably all forgotten by now (looking at you, *Coco (2017)*); Bill’s reminiscence of his care-free youth; him battling with depression and aging body; the idea that cell replacement in humans’ body was a ploy to prevent you from connecting with your body and telomeres one day would no longer get shorter, signaling eventual death. Bill may or may not have gone through all of these tragic events, but someone on Earth has gone worse and we just might never know because sonder is conceptually bizarre. So what’s the lesson here? Enjoy everything while it lasts. It’s not the mundanity that is glorified, it’s the fact that even a world of perpetual repetition beauty is not lost and tragedies empowers us. The blooming of flowers, the phenomenon of paper towel absorbing water, stars moving across the sky, colors of everything around us, driving in the night lights, a valiant reaction amidst silence. Bill’s urge to capture and savor everything he experienced before his illness swipes away his memory completely is equivalent to the saying that “living like it’s your last day.” Isn’t everything amazing because ignorance is bliss? A seemingly simple animated 2D hand-drawn movie drew out so many emotions and think-pieces like that should really be a big contender for The New Movie of the Month, don’t you think so?

<h3>July: Vada Chennai (2018)</h3>

When I visited Chennai in March to visit a friend, her friend recommended this movie to me and said that it was one of his favorites. He was an aspiring director, so I trusted him to give me a good one. He did not lie: *Vada Chennai* (Chennai Central) was a moving, entertaining, and captivating film that showcased the quintessential Tamil culture that I happened to immerse myself in, though for only a few days. For a runtime of over two and a half hours, the movie did not bore me at all; it reminded me of *City of God (2002)*, another Favorite Movie of the Month, for its brutally honest depiction of the struggling classes in the slums and their dwellers. Like the other, it also shed light on beautiful connections and cultures of these people, their endurance and belief in communities, things that truly matters and elevated these movies more than just gang fights and political disruptions. It’s humorous, light-hearted, and very Indian. A solid 4/5, not as high as others on this list, but easily the best new movie I’ve seen this month so far, and one I’ve truly enjoyed watching. Isn’t it amazing that Indian gangster thrillers are so captivating?

<h3>August: Beau Travail (1999)</h3>

The first three quarters of this movie was a bore, but then it clicked beautifully and now I have to agree that it deserves a spot in the Criterion Collection. One thing for sure, the movie was refreshing in which it isolated man in what we all agree is the most “male” environment — military — and portrayed the struggles and undercurrents that came with surviving in a male-dominated environment. If it wasn’t for this, I’d just regard it as a beautiful film, with breathtaking shots and spectacular cinematographic techniques. However, Claire Denis went above, and aboveboard built, in great subtlety, a character that is somehow bare yet complex, in a movie where words are taken over by actions and silence. Galoup, at the end of the movie, was shown bursting with emotions, the most action we’ve seen through the movie, as a last glimpse of a dying soul that was struck by jealousy and the suffer from the ubiquity and ego of the common man. When I first watched it I was bored because there was not much going on. I mean, what do you expect a bunch of military guys doing in the middle of Algeria besides doing exercises, building stuff, and linking up with locals? Shots of Galoup himself was bland then, showing him eyeing Sentain in jealousy/disdainment, dealing with his own emotions, and engaging in conversations with his superior Forestier. Many critics pointed out themes of homoeroticism, yet I wonder if it is explicit enough to be considered present. If anything, I applaud the movie for discussing the ego of heterosexual men and heteronormativity, topics that I have mentioned here and there in my posts. Toxic masculinity has suppressed men’s emotions, and with men like Galoup, envy can do dangerous things. "You’re in my power,” Galoup told Sentain earlier and followed it with, “I will destroy you” — that was superlative and serious in a way that viewers would be able to predict where the movie would steer to. Even if the movie dramatize the mentality of this type of men, isn’t it so seismically imploring how male (or human in general) emotional fragility could do such things in life, changing life trajectories forever from a nuclear moment?

<h3>October: The Substance (2024)</h3>

I drove half an hour each way to one of the only AMC movie theaters in New Jersey that shows this movie to watch what I assume is the newest super-hyped thriller on Twitter. Having experienced the same kind of situation with two other movies, namely *I Saw The TV Glow (2024)* and *Longlegs (2024)*, turned out to be quite letdowns, naturally I was more cautious this time. Turned out this is not a dud at all! On the contrary, I’d say this is (one of) the craziest, most profound, disgusting, and provoking movies I’ve ever watched, and not just this year but all time. I’m still shaking as I’m writing this before driving back to my place because my brain still could not function after watching it and I need to clear my head badly to concentrate on the road. That’s enough to guarantee this movie’s unwavering spot of the best movie of this month already even though two thirds of the month is still left. As of right now the only brain cell I’m having is literally “what the fuck is that?” Again, the most insane thing I’ve ever watched in a theater and recently, and I’ve seen a lot of things. The movie effects, characters, acting, and cinematography in this movie is just pure extravaganza, a hyperbolic yet thrilling exposure of what’s all wrong with our obsession with eternal beauty, youth, and appearance in general that are worsened by this magnifying, scrutinizing gaze of the entertainment and advertising industry. In a lot of ways, it reminded me of something that can only come from a Black Mirror episode, but more gory and bloody. Neither movies about fame is not hard to find — see A Star is Born (any version), *Perfect Blue (1997)*, *Black Swan (2011)*, *Sunset Boulevard (1957)*, nor those about beauty obsession — see *Sunset Boulevard* (again, 1957), *Human Form (2014)*, a short movie about the toxic trend of Korean teenagers abusing cosmetic surgery to deal with society’s body image obsession, and somewhat loosely *American Psycho (2000)*. 

Again, spectacular plot building, characters, acting, and makeup (and CGI whatever). Hard emphasis on the last two, because that Monster Elizasue (lol the name), was horrifying and jarring in the most positive way possible. It made me want to vomit for real, and I already wanted to twenty minutes in the movie as the bloody sacrifices Elisabeth’s body suffered from injecting, metamorphosing, and having Sue exhaust her life “juices” out of her, resulting in irredeemable scars and deformities on her body. That was enough to haunt me for days, and that was before we consider the meaning of the movie, including the definitive story of the main character Elisabeth and her younger incarnated self Sue fight against each other which eventually leads to the creation of the said monster. Her identity crisis stemmed from the growing jealousy with her copied younger self in which she kept referring as a separate identity. Sue’s (which, again, was just herself)’s reactions and revenge of almost getting terminated of the project, and the extremist abuse of the Substance that eventually ended up in the bloody gory monstrosity vividly and horrifyingly symbolized a reality that we all suffer in: the unrealistic body images, ageism, and a desire to retain youthfulness. Brands and pharmas knew this all too well and they capitalize on with anti-aging products like collagen serums and vitamin supplements just like what the Substance was marketed to be, despite not to that extent. A body horror movie is definitely a hit right in the ballpark.

<h3>November: Cool Hand Luke (1967)</h3>

*Cool Hand Luke* was added to my watchlist as I came across and learned about Paul Newman’s career. I never watched any of his movies before, so of course I decided to try out one of his most prominent ones and as soon as Tubi added it to their service this month I had to check it out. I had a habit to wait until movies about to leave streaming services would I find an incentive to watch them. I’m glad I got this one watched as I haven’t managed to decide on contenders for this month’s best new movie. There are three first impressions on this one: A) it was shot in technicolor (and I relish a sweet technicolor color-grading movie) , B) Paul Newman’s eyes are religiously tempting, and C) this is homoerotic as hell. The movie ended up darker than I thought, discussing the dark, realistic truths of the established prison system, which still ring true to this day unfortunately. With a plot that I'd say a complete opposite from *The Shawshank Redemption (1994)*, it is infuriating at times how Luke’s escapes again and again ended up failing, but as I winded down after finishing, it dawned on me. Luke cannot escape because he is not meant to be; the movie only serves to show how the human mind can withstand all woes, and somehow that’s everything. A cool hand Luke can pretend to bow down to the system, but in the end he will die a glorious death outside of the establishment instead of spending the rest of his days in it, unlike Dragline and their fellow prison mates. The movie also includes religious symbolisms, family values, and war trauma but you should see it for yourself. Also read the titular book that it was based on.

<h3>December: Die Hard (1988)</h3>

This movie is unashamedly action, and watching it on Christmas Day was a blessing for me. I don’t think I have a lot to say about it because there’s no special meaning to discuss and pure entertainment is sometimes just enough to get a five star rating in my book, if executed perfectly. The plot is nothing special, just like a typical action movie, with some bad guys and a good guy trying to do the good deed, but the nostalgic 80s cinematography makes it extra wholesome for the festive vibes everyone says it shaped up to be. A reunion gone wrong, a police propaganda and a mockery at the same time before the ACAB movement went rogue, and some heart-stopping scenes that guaranteed a classic in years to come. Die Hard deserves its reputation.
