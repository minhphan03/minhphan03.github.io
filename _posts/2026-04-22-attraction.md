It’s pretty refreshing, to be frank, to take a minute and escape the reality and immerse myself into this seemingly shallow set of pages that the short novel Bret Easton Ellis’s “The Rules of Attraction” suggested. For an American, upper middle class, 80’s decade society setting made up by bohemian adults who did not give a f about anything but sex, longing for glimpses of romance, drugs, counterculture, and everything in between, closing out the final pages managed to inspire me to write in a few sentences to recapitulate how much I love Ellis’ sense of humor and choice of words in this social satire slash critique book.

For someone who belongs in the complete opposite stereotype of the aforementioned type, there is the complete opposite a degree of nonchalance that I was impressed to the degree of quite an admiral: are these people even real? As much as I know that this is fiction, I sense a realistic inspiration when I see one — this is a social critique after all. To be freaking for real, “what is the reason?” As college students, why would they even go there, and what would they do with their future, if they even had any? I don’t even think they had a clue what they were doing in the moment. They’re like… perpetually high on drugs. Sean had sex with Paul without any recall of it ever happened because he was so pumped of drugs (recreational, of course) that Paul thought it was the real him talking and going on ‘dates’ with him (well, I just think that Paul was too naive to believe that, but who I am to judge his idiosyncratic persona?) I think it’s nutty, but within that nuttiness of the book could we exempt from the boundaries in which a book should reflect from the era in which it was written. 

The contrivance, or lack thereof, of these characters getting involved in drugs and promiscuity and infatuation was something to behold. Ellis enlivened these characters, putting on their roles by applying a childish, run-on writing style that confused me to no ends at first: how could a prolific writer type down these run-on sentences and possessed a dissent for periods just like that. He was a genius, after all. Once you manage to ignore the writing style and focus on the content and storyline, everything made sense. These characters were not your typical college students. Which is expected, because they are not real. The satirical narrative that he built for these characters were comical and eyerolling-worthy. I was not a college student back then in the 80s in the US (I wasn’t even born back then!) to verify if there was remotely someone who was as fucked-up and narcissistic as Sean Bateman or as clingy as Lauren or consumed as many drugs as Victor. 

Brent depicted a life in which nothing exists aside from a carefree, weightless idea of life, completely out of touch with the era. This disconnection, even an oblivion away from the struggle and worldly issues of the world out there outside their safe haven of a private college. Nobody would care about their majors because it was not like they would study anything at all. Paul, for once, was intelligent enough to not have to engage in studying too much (I guess). He did not fail his classes a lot, at least from what I remembered. He went back and forth between smoking, partying, flirting with boys (saying he was bisexual, but damn if we knew if he was genuinely interested in girls. Lauren was his girlfriend once, but they parted ways for no clear reason), and doing anything else but studying. He yearned for uncertain scenarios: a relationship with Sean, having sex with guys on campus, panting after “blond-tan-good-body-stupid-goons,” and not fixing his relationship with his mother at all. He was the most charismatic and sound-minded for sure, at least between the three characters, as he had quite a character development throughout the book. He knew that pursuing Bateman was pointless, so he left. He knew it was better than to stay with Lauren, so he left. He knew he couldn’t solve his mommy issues, so he left.

And then there’s Lauren. What do you mean she swapped majors whenever she broke up with a boy, like her future depended on how satisfied she was with her dating life? She also got stuck in gossip about who fucks who, engaged in those conversations and fed into some more, constantly comparing everyone she brought into bed with her ex, and thought Sean would be her getaway ticket from those haunted dreams. She suffered from the consequence of abortion as it fell upon the young girl when Sean got sober and broke up with her (he asked to marry her, just so you know) out of the blue on their trip to New York. She did not love him, anyway, so who cares. 

Sean, an ex-jock, harbored a contempt towards that life and his resolution living a bohemian life in a liberal arts college made so much sense because of that. His dad fell ill, but he rarely cared. Living such a carefree life like that, deep down he was an empty hopeless soul. We knew he tried to kill himself many times with hanging or cutting himself with a razor when Lauren wanted to break up with him because he slept with another girl, so once he believed he found a reason to live (this assumed obsession with Lauren), he tried so hard to not get shot by Rupert. In classes, he was so drugged and high he thought about anything but the lecture he was at: he looked at this girl and thought of Lauren, then suppressed his urge to jerk off because he didn’t get to finish that morning, then conjured a one-way beef with other students in the class just because they raised their hands. He got nauseous thinking about Lauren hanging out with Vittorio, a professor in the college whom Lauren performed oral sex on (ugh). He gave no shit to anything, because as soon as he got high, he forgot everything, and when he got sober, he forgot what he was doing when he was high. His moment of clarity after his post-nut clarity was wild as hell: he did not remember his last time making love to somebody sober. Rock n’ roll, I guess.

In this pretty posh, elite, and white college environment at Camden College, bohemians like these three characters live without a care in the world, because everywhere you go you meet somebody who is as fucked-up as you. Everything you do is partying, sexing, gossiping, or dealing drugs or wallowing in the dark emotions of missing your boyfriends or the most trivial kind of things. Granted that this work is fictional and there is no way that an actual college advisor would let somebody get away with failing three courses (and Sean thought it was still better than what he hoped for?), don’t you think it’s really ridiculous how this college actually functioned? A college setting with barely any administration. 

This book insisted on reminding me that it was written in the last century: the author mentioned some of the items or activities the society nowadays would scratch their heads at, like slipping quarters into jukeboxes, making tapes, listening to Springsteen (well, I can assure you that no typical teen today would listen to Springsteen), and having Iggy Pop playing in the radio (mind you, he started his career in the 1960s!). This dedication to contemporary culture was nostalgic to read: it reminded me of the simpler days, which go cheek-to-cheek with the unserious minds of the characters in the book. At the same time, these boys and girls were timeless: they were broken free of the pressing matters at the time that uniquely defined what it means to live in the 1980s: no HIV/AIDS, no Vietnam War, and gayness was blooming in the fields, like forks found in the kitchen. It felt fresh and retrospective in a fresh and comedic way that allured me to finish the book in one go, which was a rare feat given how rarely I read books (I only read them out of boredom, and even then I wouldn’t finish one and remember a single detail from it). 

One last interesting detail about the book was how messy the writing was (I already mentioned how the run-on sentences were a signature, but I thought I would give it a more direct shout-out). Given an example: ‘The townies would watch us suspiciously, Sean shooting pool, drinking beer, me shuffling over to the jukebox, slipping quarters in it, punching F17, the first strains coming on shuffling back to where Sean sat, now by the bar, motorcycle helmets propped up by our drinks, and he’d lip-synch it” (Paul). And to be considered that Paul was the smartest one out of them, it was just so damn genius how the characters narrated their lives. Ellis knew what it was living that life, a life where words just get thrown freely without any serious weight to it. The characters lived their lives in this bubbled "freedom" in which those words would be as bare and simple as they would be. No thoughts, head empty. All of these words may probably mean nothing and all of these events may just be conjured up in one character's cooked-as-fuck brain. Who knows?
