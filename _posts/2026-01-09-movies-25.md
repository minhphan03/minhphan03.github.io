This year, I started to get more into books, which further reduced my time spent watching and the number of movies I watched this year compared to 2024. It does not mean that I love movies any less: like the previous year-end movie post, I spent more time revisiting movies, saddened by the fact that many details have been forgotten over time. I ended up rewatching them with reinvigorated appreciation and newfound thoughts that were almost as if I were watching them for the first time. With that being said, this list still focuses on the latter, but that may or may not change in the future. Let's dive into it (and beware for spoilers... you've been warned)!

<h3>January: From Ground Zero: Stories from Gaza (2024)</h3>

A collection of shorts directed by Palestinians living in Gaza during the occupation under Israeli forces, the shorts reveal unprecedented insights into the struggles, hopes, dreams, and history of the innocent people whose lives have turned for the worse ever since the fateful October 17th, 2023. I watched it in theaters with a few people, and it was heartbreaking to see how fragile a person can be portrayed, more so because this is technically a real documentary and not some kind of historical drama depicting war. Those people were physical beings praying to survive another day. I recalled the donation funds and campaigns on GoFundMe that were organized by Gazans who were imploring for assistance to pay for food: food aid was simply not enough, and with limited signal reception they needed to do anything to live another day, and hoped that one day they were able to see peace again, to reunite with their family members, to sleep a peaceful sleep. A lot of these shorts focus on children, which were already heartbreaking to witness, but also anecdotes of fully grown adults whose dreams were crushed and they were too emotionally distraught to do anything: a man having to steal a body bag to sleep; an aspiring filmmaker having to resort to filming about the ongoing genocide because lives were too at stake for him to pursue filming one that he actually wants (**Sorry Cinema**); a woman reusing her precious water to cook, wash her children, do laundry, and watering crops (**Recycling**); an old taxi driver offering free transport service using his donkey, then getting martyred (**Taxi Wannessa**). However, we still see glimpses of hope: a puppet show for children (**Awakening**), a stand-up comedy show (**Everything is Fine**), and an amateur a cappella music group singing about the day of freedom (**No**). I later learned that "ground zero" stands for the area marked by nuclear bombs, which made the documentary more powerful. What was going on (and still is) in Gaza is a modern atomic bomb disaster, killing millions of people, that we just don't speak out and condemn those who enabled it in the beginning enough. More thoughts are expanded in [this post](https://www.minhph.com/2025/10/17/from-ground-zero).

<h3>February: I'm Still Here (2024)</h3>

Based on the real-life events of the Paiva family during Brazil's military dictatorship in 1964, it is a violent, courageous, and inspiring story. Fernanda Torres's acting was top-notch, transcendental, and moving in this beautiful drama about the hidden and brave sacrifice of motherhood. All hidden, underlying the brave countenance of a mother who has to put on a mask in front of her children to hide away the fact that her beloved husband was taken away under the "forced disappearance." Torres disappeared into the role, captivating my heart with her portrayal of the struggle of a distraught wife desperate to find out her husband's fate and whereabouts while navigating her children's concerns, confusion, and well-being as they grow up in an environment where the truth and freedom have been taken away. I thought the last piece of her later years of life, where she battled with her Alzheimer's, was unnecessary at first; however, I looked back upon it and now realize its vitality in highlighting the permanent scars of these political disruptions on innocent lives — loneliness, lost memories, and what-ifs. Having her children surrounding her was heart-warming, but deep inside her heart, despite the Alzheimer's, we can clearly sense the lingering longing and emptiness, despite her blank looks that were showcased on the screen. Also, a shout-out to the beautiful cinematography and the movie's score: so enchanting, nostalgic for the vibrant Brazilian society of the 60s, where vinyl records and cultural exchange thrived beyond boundaries.

<h3>March: Before Sunrise (1995)</h3>

A beautiful, emotional, impactful movie about love and chances of love. Talking to a stranger on a train can lead you to the most incredible night of your life. The magic of human connection is the space between us, which is the God we've always been looking for. It is our ability to connect and synchronize our hearts, coming together to collectively feel, sympathize, and understand each other as one. Give yourself to another person, for only one momentary night, and see how it can deliver a lasting impact. You know you would never see each other again. Still, you gave the best you had because the transient life itself urges for more bursting, spontaneous, crazy moments to justify its existence, like meeting a person that changed how you view the world, or a movie that leaves a lasting impression on you. What could have been and what has been, I asked myself that question so many times. How many times have I regretted not doing something I could have done or talking to someone? I loved how this movie delved into answering it. The conversations between the two of them could have been silly or dragging at times, but for those moments, how they talked about life and the existential need to express oneself and want to be returned in some kind of way, were worth watching. Tore my heart really badly at the end when they promised to see each other again in six months because deep down they knew they would never make it, but they did it anyway because the human connection knew what the best course to do in every single moment was. They wanted to see each other again because they knew this night they had was magical and their relationship was special. If they never made that promise, they would regret it for the rest of their lives. The transient life should make you cherish the moments you have and give you the urge to never take them for granted. An excellent movie, providing high-quality entertainment and great food for thought.

<h3>April: Dial M for Murder (1954)</h3>

If there is not a single enjoyer of Technicolor, consider I'm dead. One of the elements that elevates this movie is the Technicolor grading, which has nothing to do with the movie whatsoever. The technology is present and utilized, whether the movie is good or not, but damn if I said what the element that made this movie edge out from another contender, *Black Bag (2025)* was, it's this. Despite the plot and setting, which are simple and straightforward, just like many other Hitchcock movies (*Rope (1948)*, *Rear Window (1954)*), the movie remains as spellbinding and fascinating as ever. A truly prominent and distinctive style that no other director can fully replicate.

<h3>May: Mission: Impossible (1996)</h3>

The one that paved the way and established one of the most successful and iconic franchises of all time, *Mission: Impossible* was an easy watch and jam-packed with eyefuls of satisfying action and thrill. It had enough baits and switches and turns that kept the ball rolling and the audience's eyes glued to the screen. Similar to *Die Hard (1988)*, which I introduced as the Movie of the Month for December, it was one of those movies that I had no choice but to watch in one sitting without pausing. It was simply delicious, irresistible, and spellbinding. The cinematography was very 90s-coded, which added to the nostalgia and the authentic feel that classic movies often evoke: not swamped with green screens and CGI, and such special effects. I'm not talking down about movies that used them, but when Tom Cruise climbed to the top of that train, it felt much more realistic than if the movie had better quality (in terms of resolution). Not to confuse anyone with the fact that films used to have backdrops for car scenes (where a person would drive in a car in a studio and the background would be some moving street to give the illlusion that they were filming the vehicle actually moving) and the fake-ness was totally discernible, the fact that these scenes are more blurry and low quality give an impression that they was actually filmed by a camera and not digitally edited using probably two hundred different computers.

<h3>June: Paris Is Burning (1990)</h3>

It has been a long while since I last watched a documentary, and *Paris Is Burning* was worth the watch: it was inspiring, extraordinary, humane, and heartbreaking. Queer history, for most of the time, has overlooked minor ethnicities, and Black queer history most of all. In the documentary, we see how vogue and ball culture emerged and were popularized as a response to this cultural repression. A reverie, an escape from the heteronormative norms and expectations, Black queer people's expression grew larger than life and at its brightest on the dance floors and outside on the streets with their chosen families. As part of Twitter culture, I acknowledge how this culture permeates the medium, infiltrating everyday life and influencing contemporary discourses and communication styles. Watching this documentary just confirmed it for me. It aged like fine wine due to the fact that much of the lexicon and diction younger generations speak today is so heavily influenced by and incorporated from these cultures that we never batted an eye at the fact that we were appropriating; furthermore, queer people still struggle today, even if the world seems to have been more open to them. Living in the dark and fears of a cruel world, to us, has unfortunately never gone away. Anecdotes from Venus Xtravaganza and Octavia St. Laurent still apply to someone in this day and age. I admire how Jennie Livingston highlighted and celebrated the lives of these icons. Although some of them never survived, their legacies live on forever.

<h3>July: Superman (2025)</h3>

Watching this in IMAX at the theater was a genuinely satisfying and memorable experience for me. I was so hyped for this movie because the trailer was promising, and I'm not even a trailer person. Twitter hype really sold me on this one, because this is the first time I've ever watched a Superman movie. I did not have any preconceptions before coming, as I didn't watch superhero movies frequently, so fatigue was not an issue for me. I believe that's why I had such a fantastic time at the theater. Corenswet was charming and dripping with charisma; his portrayal of Superman totally bewitched me. His chemistry with Brosnahan was off the charts, and the action sequences were fulfilling. It was hopeful, exciting, and checked all the boxes of what I thought a signature superhero movie would entail. Hoult's performance was also praiseworthy, and the trio really sold me on this one. To believe that the only DC movies I've ever watched are Aquaman and its sequel, and they didn't impress me that much, this universe's reboot attempt, led by James Gunn, who is also the movie's director, has gained my attention and will have my unwavering support. And yes, I will watch more Superman movies, I will…

<h3>August: Weapons (2025)</h3>

In August, I decided to rewatch more movies, so the few new movies that stood out as strong contenders are not as competitive. With that being said, *Weapons* was a good one: it's captivating, the plot is fresh, and the plot twists are believable. I'm not a big fan of horror or thrillers, so even though the movie pulls me in at the right time, it automatically earns four stars and above. <h3>September: Le Samouraï (1967)</h3>

September was even worse for new movies because school had started, and I had little time to watch movies. Additionally, my local Regal theater had a "Month of Masterpieces" program that featured many old movie reruns. Therefore, I basically spent a lot of time just reliving old favorites on the big screen. However, I happened to watch the neo-noir *Le Samouraï*, and it was truly phenomenal. The moody inner life of the main character was portrayed on screen perfectly, with Technicolor capturing the vibrant nature of Paris society in the 1960s, in contrast to Jef Costello's (Alain Delon) somber, dark suits and even more so with his muted, expressionless countenance. Using his solitude and somewhat rundown living conditions, in contrast to the chatty, upper-class crowd and suited men in the bars and clubs, we see a shift to more intricate filmmaking that describes the introspection of human life, a quality NOT not previously seen but also not yet fully realized. There is an internal struggle, there is an obsession with order in the impassive hitman, yet he never shows it. He did not say much, but his eye movements showed every emotion. Alain Delon and Cathy Rosier were so captivating and skillful in their acting in this movie; it's plain incredible. Furthermore, the movie's pace was immaculate, gripping you tightly at times and letting you loose to observe the background of the scenes and trail behind the characters in ways that reminded me of later movies I've watched, such as *The Conversation (1974)* or *Chinatown (1974)*. Just an awesome, well-rounded, and timeless classic.

<h3>October: Children of Men (2006)</h3>

For a movie that started out on the wrong foot for me (I found it somewhat confusing and grim), it only got better and better as it progressed. What stood out to me was the gripping, uniquely captivating, continuous one-shot scenes, where the camera alternated between the characters and what they were looking at. I thought that was cool as heck because I hadn't paid attention to this factor in filmmaking until I watched it — and wow, it did make a big difference to the viewing experience. On the other hand, the plot was tight, the world-building was quite lovely, and the military/political message was worth the watch. I was dreading that it would somewhat be a clone of *28 Days Later (2002)* with all of this futuristic British ruins and mass upheaval (no offense, it's just because I wasn't a big fan of that movie at all). Still, the message definitely lifts the whole watching experience, gave me food for thought, and had me looking up for analyses online after the credits started rolling.

<h3>November: Bugonia (2025)</h3>

I watched a record-low number of movies this month, with only six, and half of them were already rewatches. It does not mean that *Bugonia* is just an okay watch — it was very masterfully crafted. Emma Stone is excellent as always, and the movie becomes more interesting as it progresses. I mean, I always expected Yorgos Lanthimos' films to be a little bit outrageous, but this one is thankfully within my enjoyment range, like *Poor Things (2023)*.

<h3>December: Zootopia 2 (2025)</h3>

I can't recall the last time I considered a PG-rated, children-targeted animated movie a personal monthly favorite, but *Zootopia 2* genuinely deserves this spot. The plot was quite original, and the flow was excellent. I mean, an animal society is definitely not a new concept. Still, at least this time the writers were able to expand the universe and build a believable, natural development to the story, making it easy to catch up even if you don't remember the first part (which isn't the case for many franchises). I had a great time at the theater watching this with my friends, and not every movie experience can be said the same. With that being said, I personally prefer it if the romance innuendos were more subtle and should be interpreted by the audience themselves. Still, at the same time, it was carried out rather nicely… that was sweet and not overshadowing the movie itself (thankfully), while leaving at least something for people to talk about. The after-credits scene excited me a little bit too…
