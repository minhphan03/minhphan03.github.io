I have never been this more hesitant to rate a movie like this one. One thing I'm sure of is that if I watch this at home, I would rate it as low as possible because of how long and slow it is. But its slowness is a videographic technique: slow cinema is a real thing and breathing. I would never bought this up if I did so; I watched it in the theater. I tried to justify its cinemagraphy with a somewhat desperate effort to make sense of the excitement I had for the movie when it debuted in the domestic theaters. At the end, I thought that it was more interesting than I thought it would, despite my countless mental checkins and checkouts I suffered throughout the three hour runtime. Its Christianity elements was a turnoff to me, and faith suddenly felt much more alienated. But after all it's still an interesting one. Here are some random details and thoughts I had after watching it,

1. Conspiracy: When Thiện went to Mr. Lưu's house on his bike with Đạo, he deliberatedly tried to slow down. I thought at first maybe he did it only for the video crew to follow behind, but maybe it was also an aftereffect of the devasting accident of Đạo's mom. The child was already traumatized enough after it, and Thiện, as his uncle, wanted to protect the child from the danger out there that could happen at any time (?)

2. Conspiracy: The bird burial in juxtaposition to the growing green sprout. In the scene where Thiện and Đạo buried the dead bird, we saw the flaying green sprout at one corner. In one moment lasting around five seconds, it was the only moving creature, in contrast with the unmovable animal that represented Thiện's hope when he picked it up in front of the hospital where his sister in law passed away. Somewhat it represents the existential nihilism reincarnation theory: things keep moving, and life is weightless and bound to die at one point. The bird, when it died, went back to its earth and another one would come about from the ruins. That sprout would appear anywhere and grow up from that bird, continuing its existence in a way or another.

3. Conspiracy: Other contrasts in the same frames, such as the intimate, retrospective conversation between Mr. Lưu & Thiện versus the constant ongoing echoes of the broadcast news, or the individual unbothered with a phone in hand amidst the funeral, or the sound of the ticking clock drowning out the spiritual conversation between Thiện and Đạo made so much sense after the end of the movie. Life goes on and on like a clockwork, and only by playing fairly with its rules can you stay sane. Sometimes you need to stop and think about its ruthless pace, and for once enjoy the simplicity of abiding by it. Him coming home was not only to go back to his roots, but to also searching for a meaning of everything. When he drowned himself in the stream at the end of the movie, he realized everything: nothing really matters. The water keeps running and running, and all his foe would soon drift away as he dies. In this somewhat existentially nihilistic understanding, Thiện tried so hard to make sense of it, of the reason why his girlfriend living him to return to God and him moving to the city to find a good job for himself. At last, epiphanies were there to grasp: faith in life is a faith in purity, in the simplicity of itself, and doing towards the good. 

4. Conspiracy: Lacks of cuts: one of the notable traits of Inside the Yellow Cocoon Shell is its usage of one shots (one of the scenes is an half-an-hour one-shot) in contrast with Thiện's job as a video editor/videographer. We could see during one of the scenes that he was working on his editing app, and there was a lot of cuts. This movie does not have that many.

5. Opinion: Thiện ignoring the accident and him actually getting involved: This is so genius, honestly. By cutting off that peaceful, trivial conversation with a mood-breaking event, it offers three notions about life: Human life is transient (physical death), luck can be a curse (Đạo survived, but he suddenly became an orphan), and chance works in the most unpredictable ways (the accident he ignored actually involved hímelf).

6. Opinion: Wedding and funeral: We got to see the glimpses of Thiện recording a wedding ceremony before attending the funeral of his sister in law. This cannot be a clearer juxtaposition, can it? Also, the white butterfly outside the praying sessions.

7. Opinion: Yellow cocoon shells: The director explained that the title symbolizes "for people trapped chasing after fame or fortune and to escape the "shell" requires some soul-searching revolving around the question of 'What to live for'." At the same time they refer to the silkworm shells used for silk production in sericulture, as we could see a scene when Thiện "visited" his brother's house in Di Linh Village. Only by breaking through this barrier and relinquish the obsession with fame or fortune could people live freely, just like silkworms or butterflies breaking out of their shells to continue their life cycle. For Thiện, it was for finding himself. Drowned in the ups and downs in the city, he lost himself after the traffic tragedy, and going back to his hometown in the mountains was the beginning of his journey to find his faith. His faith, at times, dappered in Christian values, but towards the end of the movie it became more than that: Christianity becomes family, God becomes protector, and his question of Entity in the facets of modern society becomes a spiritual belief in the self, the transformation of one.

8. Opinion: White butterflies: The white butterflies scene towards the end of the movie as Thiện wandered in the dark forest symbolizes somewhat a popular saying: nothing comes without sacrifice. White butterflies itself stood for purity and innocence. That was what he was looking for. In the journey of finding himself found Thiện encountered hardships, but only by going through it himself could he see through the meaning of it all: this is not a journey to find his brother; it is a journey to find his ego, his self.

9. Opinion: The conversation with the old woman with Alzheimer's: I remembered that she was talking about how human life was transient in the grand scheme of everything, and all angels who wished to return to earth are sufferable. Once you arrive at Heaven and get rid of the physical dying self, you leave behind the suffering that humans have to face everyday. You don't know how there is so much more outside there from the cocoon of money and life and fortune, and until the day you no longer get used to living in this cocoon would you discover that universal truth. The woman likens it to the case of people at the wet market: they were so used to the putrid air of dead fish and blood that they didn't think it was such a "putrid" thing anymore. But those "angels," they would suffer to dead if they see those scenes.

10. Opinion: The dream: Thiện dreamed of 'going to meet his brother' and not 'getting to meet,' which was interesting. People always idealizes the prospect of something happening, but often regret or get let down by the actual event. Even in his dreams he could not get to see his brother. He got to see the woman that brought lunch to his brother, but when they came down to the working site, she disappeared. In his consciousness he was not ready to face the man. What would he said when he actually see him again? Would the conversations flow nicely or awkwardly? We don't know, but one thing for sure we can see how the movie re-emphasize the meaning of finding self, and this failure culminated everything that happened in the three hours of the movie: not everything is going to work your way, so live a life that you feel free and pure. So he drowned himself in the stream, washing away the woes and miseries, and in a way, revived himself.
