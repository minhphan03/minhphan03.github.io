What do they all have in common?

Race. They all relate to race. I know it’s tiring to talk about race in this blog, but race is so deeply rooted in the US society that it renders impossible to NOT talk about it. Systemized racism, that’s the word. If there is one thing that is irreversibly broken, it’s racism. We try to address it, to cover it and ameliorate its consequences, but in the deepest corners of the back of our minds, we know it persists, like an incurable disease in this society. It is generational, atrocious, and traumatizing. It defines and controls our decisions all the time.

Ken Burns' documentary on the Vietnam War further skewed the public reception, I stand for that statement. What does it even mean, I suppose, to promise an unbiased (impartial) perspective on the war but never come close enough to actually do it? I had to read Le Ly Hayslip's "When Heaven and Earth Changed Places" for a class and the book reeked white supremacy despite the author herself constantly denied its influences in her tone. There are some vibes, you know? She talked about how the American GIs treated her and fellow Vietnamese women, and it was somewhat very biased. There was a part in the book where she recalled an encounter with two GIs when they talked about hooking up with her as a gift for their “service” in the country before they flew back to the US. She knew it was wrong to treat a human like a commodity, but White Man’s Burden ideology has rotten many brains that she came to accept it was normalcy whatsoever. My Lai massacre was another story. I came across a tweet on this topic a couple days ago of a photo of a group of women and children looking tremendously disturbed and disoriented, and the author pointed out a girl buttoning up her blouse in the back. I shuddered. I knew it all too well what it meant. They were sexually assaulted before that picture. SEXUALLY ASSAULTED. Was there even a more disturbing scene on Earth comparing to this? Tragically, after the photo was taken, the group of villages were shot. I understand there is two side to the story, just in case anyone wants to rebut; however, this is just plain inhumane. People’s lives were grass, and “search and destroy” mission, I wonder, was just a facade for these (white) GIs to exploit and harass the people out of the sexual racism we never really talk about?

Cathy Park Hong mentioned the War in her book “Minor Feelings.” It was a heavy read for sure, but it’s a captivating one. She talked about how Asian men had to serve for their “country,” a country that was (and still is) so infested of white supremacy, and betrayed their Asian roots going to search and destroy the Vietnamese revolutionary forces, whom were also suffering from these bigoted, self-centered military superiors. Now it leads me to think about myself, a Vietnamese living in this said ex-rival country at this very moment. Fortunately, it’s not a matter of survival anymore, but through time and time I have observed and thought a lot about micro-agressions lately. I had my fair share of encountering these little issues in everyday life. Cathy being an Asian woman told me a lot of things about what it felt like to be one: how she got segregated at school, in her own community, and how the Asian community dealt with racial remarks over the years — we are used as the middlemen to push black people down; that our feelings are invalid because “we’re the next in line” (to White people, in terms of popularity), and almost completely invisible when it comes to racial debates. we’re duped as compliant and quiet, as hard-workers and alien followers. Even in our communities we step on others as we try to prove our worth in a caucasian centric society, blinded by the white beauty standards: long artificial eyelashes, v-line jaws, high-bridged noses, pale skins, etc. I’m both terrified and dejected looking at how both native Asians and Caucasians look down on these natural characteristics just because they do not conform to that beauty, and internalize that conformity to traumatize and pass down to generations and generations of minorities. Through war, Westernization, and popular media, we lose authenticity and move towards a monotonic society where herd mentality destroys one’s confidence and bears trauma on their identities. 

As a gay man, this phenomenon is even more alarming. Everywhere I look, every place I go, I sense the dominance of white gay (not queer) culture thick in the air: Tom of Finland’s illustrations project a hypermasculine yet erotic/sensual representation of queer men that are nothing short of protruding muscles and crotches; OnlyFans, CrossFit, Grindr, and fashion culture promote a generic ideology of what a dream man is appearance-wise. The wavelike repercussions reconstruct and reaffirm what I believe should be a diverse, endless, freeform community into the hands and images of a few. Queer men should be slender, fat, old, young, Asian, White, Hispanic, and everybody in between. Is it because of how estranged and ostracized we have been in the heteronormative world that instead of bringing up the marginalized population of our very special community, we ruthlessly tear them down as an attempt to fit in within a selfish world. The struggle of queer men, the story of who they are meant to be, is a tale as old as time, isn’t it? Brokeback Mountain, Alan Turing, Moonlight, Freddie Mercury, etc. are among the popular cultural staples in queer media, but what do they all have in common? These characters, these people, they all end up in misery and despair. We want power, but what attempted for beautiful homages to who we are came out as pity and tragic. Marginalized people have to either be pitied on or subvert their innermost identities to gain control and power, and it hurts to admit the truth. 

Vietnam War bore trauma for generations, but it is just one of many instances of impairity we have seen or unconsciously felt. Being women sucks in more ways than one, but how many people are confident enough to fight against the rigid bigotry men has set in stone for thousands of years? We are assaulted in more ways than one, even when you’re living in what others would call peace. It is suffocating, dire, and jarring. We can change ourselves to fit in, but at the deepest corners of ourselves, are we simply covering poorly that said trauma? And how longer we can bear these scars?
