*Written as a midterm submission for English 207 - Introduction to Cultural Studies at University of Washington - Seattle*

As a realist, I stopped believing in the existence of mythological creatures long ago (until proved otherwise); however, I’m curious about how we, collectively as a society, have grown so obsessive (and unfortunately infatuated) over these creatures. Is it that the world of vampires has been so closely connected to our own over the years that stopping people from reading about them seems impossible?

To answer this question, remind yourself that vampires were mythical creatures and products of the human mind. Their appearances and behaviors in folklore and early published media were ill-advised: drinking human or animal blood, killing naive villagers, or living independently and nocturnally. In Eastern European folktales, they were ‘reported’ sexually harassing widows, leading to vampire hunting and massacres of suspicious individuals. In Asian countries, vampirical creatures and similar beings also play significant roles in such alienation practices, such as the Phillipian Tagalog Mandurugo (‘bloodsucker’) or Malaysian Langsuir. They embedded what considered destructive behaviors, and what was better than creating dangerous creatures to scare people away from performing ‘bad’ actions?

It was not until the 18th century that vampire tales became more well-known across the globe throughout publications. However, the depiction of these creatures still bore heavy resemblances to folklore. Varney the Vampire, a collection of penny dreadfuls, introduced the mass population to the image of individual vampires feeding on commoners. Carmilla, the female vampire in the 1872 novella, was portrayed as an anti-sentimental, cold-blooded, and merciless killer. But perhaps the most iconic out of them all must be the iconic ‘Dracula’ by Bram Stoker. Setting itself up as an embodiment of Victorian sexuality, gothic culture, bloodcurdling horror, and newfound yet intriguing fears, Count Dracula perpetuated into the popular culture and ever since then redefined what it means to be a true ‘vampire’: most likely having no reflection, or fear of crucifixes, or the capability to turn victims into their own kind.

No matter how different these characters are from each other, they are all somewhat humans but not entirely humans. Over time, we have mastered and practiced the idea of vampires to the point that the fear of the unknown and herd immunity plays such an essential role in perpetuating such features. The vampires in these stories could act just like humans, yet they harm humans without remorse. Societies back then, without any means of effective communications like we have today, primarily relied on their own culture to protect their originality. Science was primitive, and we did not have the answer to every possible external threat, such as diseases, back then. So desperate were such attempts to find the sources of these threats that they started to develop tales to justify their origins. Vampirical stories were among them. Again, what was better than using sexuality and death (dead bodies carry diseases) to even bump up more of their ‘infective’ and ‘deadly’ behaviors? The consequences were clear: look at how society at that time dealt with them through vampire hunting. Since they could blend in with the mass, any ‘abnormal’ being would be disgustingly mistreated. Violence was deemed acceptable to fellow humans as a means to prevent these threats and elicit strength, both mentally and physically. The human race’s disturbing concept that we are the superior species was reflected by how we have monstrously depicted these creatures using the fears, imperfections, and sins we encounter in everyday life. Through these tales, fighting these demons regain our position and inspire people to grow past the shortcomings.

As science advances, most people stop believing in these tales. We realized that diseases and epidemics had nothing to do with these creatures but rather from humans themselves. Therefore, their primary use resorted to teaching (and intimidating) children about dangers and norms or research on society and cultures. Also, with sexuality becoming less taboo and more liberating and freedom of speech more encouraged, vampires take on new roles as fantasy subjects. Authors and moviemakers redeem their images by embracing their supernatural powers, with Twilight and Vampire Diaries as two symbolic candidates, let alone countless fanfics and similar motif stories. They more or less explore their ‘humane’ side and selectively single out traits that glorify and contribute to alternate worlds where their presence would make significant differences to human society. Quick adaptation to globalization and technological changes allows newer generations, especially teenagers, to challenge their parents’ views and adopt radical, progressive beliefs, and these creatures come in a new light. Dracula now holds more as a figure of power, mystery, and affluence than just a bloodsucker. It was even romanticized to fit into the oversaturated escapism theme these days. The adaptation of Carmilla into a mini-series saw a shift from her fearsome aura to a love-hate homoerotic relationship, which closely fit the thirst for teenage drama content at that time. Contemporary audiences, as cultural entities, consume these products and internalize a completely alienated view on vampires, compared to what people had been bad-mouthing them for hundreds of years earlier. And I think it’s exciting to see how we will develop them in the future.
