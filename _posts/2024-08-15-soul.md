I watched "Soul" for the first time shortly after its release, and I did not remember much of it aside from a disdainful impression that it was very forgettable. I was very wrong. I rewatched it tonight, and I wonder if I was too young to understand the profound meaning behind the movie and that maybe it was one of the best original feature films Pixar has released. The plot is super original, and the adventure that Joe and 22 embarked on together, with the purpose of reviving Joe himself, while simultaneously searching for a life purpose for a directionless, witty 22, was magical, brimming with worldly lessons, and emotional. It is been a while since I was so immersed in a movie for the entire first hour, so to say that it is an achievement is an understatement.

Throughout the movie, we saw Joe’s desperation to "come back" (revive) himself after falling into the strange land between death and the afterlife. As a result, he missed out on the definitive opportunity to perform with popular jazz musician Dorothea Williams. Still, through that incident, he was able to find out what life was worth. Resurrected as a cat instead of himself, he was forced to take matters into his own hands and guide 22 in navigating life in New York City while discovering himself from the perspective of an outsider. The story of his barber friend, Dez, hinted at this idea. Despite having a dream of being a veterinarian, Dez was unfortunately forced to give up on it and settle down to be where he was. Still, he was not disappointed in the slightest. To him, life has more to offer than what he once aspired to do. In other words, a spark does not define someone’s path in life. This was reiterated in Jerry’s words: “Your spark isn’t your purpose. That last box fills in when you’re ready to come live.” Before landing on Earth and entering a baby, a new soul has to fill in a “spark,” which, in my own terms, may mean a proper, valid reason to "serve" on Earth. Everyone was born to not be nothing, so in that case, a spark may guide them through, along with their personalities, in the first steps of life. However, life is random (and in this movie, only happens once, no recurrence ad infinitum), so one’s lightness should only stem from accepting all the facets of life and the randomness it brings. Joe falling down a manhole was an unpredictable event, yet from that event, he learned so much about himself and about the desire to just “live.”

In a way, this movie answered so much about my current identity crisis at the age of 20. It was not until now that I thought about it for the first time (if you read my previous posts, you can easily point that out). I first proactively chewed over it when I watched “Handsome Devil” (2016). I hit play on that movie expecting it to be for laughs, as a lighthearted movie for a weekend night to just chill and relax. It turned out to be more serious than that. Unbeknownst to the film crew, I found the movie not quite what they hoped I would… it was not a masterpiece, as much as that “serious” scene led me to believe, and so it was not. I found it to be badly executed in a way that the hackneyed tropes and predictable plot details squashed any excitement I had. However, there was this one beautiful quote that ignited my interest in evaluating how terrible my identity crisis had been. At the beginning of the movie, Andrew Scott, in the role of a queer English teacher, quoted: “You spend your whole life being someone else; who’s going to be you?”

“Soul” reminded me of this movie somehow. I do not know why I thought of it while watching the other, but I’m always glad to draw some connections between different movies as an expansion of my “cinematic universe.” I think it resonates strongly with my situation during this period of downtime, where I do not actively participate in college life anymore, and I temporarily lost the only identity I associated myself with: being a college student. Graduation has its prices. I do not know what I am doing with my life now that I do not do homework anymore or work part-time at the library where I had been working for two years. At least I still do some outside tech projects that give me some sense of value and self-worth. I know I should not tether myself to these external influences and instead rummage through my inner self for much-needed stability. In this day and age, It is not easy to just do that. I got overexposed to social media and its anxiety-inducing tactics, and now that I have nothing to do, I cannot help but lean further into it. I wanted so badly to be like those people I admired, with great jobs, memorable experiences, and exciting travel. I talked to my therapist endlessly about the same topic, growing afraid she would tire of reiterating her opinions over and over again, only for me to retreat back to my old ways because I’m too much of a man-child or too pig-headed to attempt any reinvention. I write these words down, but I doubt I will remember them three months later. She recently told me how it took almost half of her 20s to figure out what to do with her life, from a BA in English to years of serving at a fine restaurant, which she loved, and a dead-end child services job that kindled her passion for therapy and pushed her to pursue a Master’s degree in Psychology. She told me that life is not a straight path that you can force to your will, and only by bumping into different aspects of it can you maybe find what you love.

This is also the lesson that Joe learned throughout the movie: you do not need a clear, monumental purpose in life to be a winner in the rat race called life; simply a passion to live and experience could be enough as you find your true identity. Conor Masters, the closeted titular character in “Handsome Devil”, underwent a period of self-oppression before he broke through the misogynistic, patriarchal ideals to truly express who he was meant to be. “Who’s going to be you?” is not just a rhetorical question alluding to self-identification. It is an exasperation, a desire to learn and unlearn what is pushed upon oneself. For Joe to unlearn that his only sole pathway to success is to become a jazz artist, and for 22 to unlearn that there is no “spark” on Earth that would entice her to begin her life, we realize that maybe we all need a shake-up, a period of upheaval to disrupt and deconstruct our pigheaded, dogmatic beliefs, those that we are too proud of to relinquish. We all live in our bubbles of absolute convenience and are afraid to take risks, and I know because I am the biggest example of it all. I guess “to come live” is to take your opportunities to push yourself to the most uncomfortable limits, as long as they are within your limits (like, do not go insane).

For instance, I am, you could say, someone who is always afraid of everything: financial instability, dependence on parents, figuring things out on my own, joblessness. I troubled my loved ones enough and tore my pride too much to have the mental capacity to put myself into any more uncomfortable situations, but at the same time, I feel like I am nowhere near there yet. I guess that is because I need to learn more about this world, and with the little time I have before graduation, when I will be able to truly start doing what I want to do, I am torn between two pieces of myself: a part of me wants to handle more adult responsibilities, and the other part wants to just get it done and over with. It is easier said than done that your life is yours to experience, because what is implied is that you one hundred percent control your fate and who you want to be, and that is simply not true in my case. Years of trauma and yearning have fettered my willingness to take risks and experience life, and instead led me to take a more grounded and carefully controlled perspective on the people around me.

Joe’s story would never have turned out the way it did if it weren’t for that little adventure, would it? Maybe I need something like that, something to shake the foundation to the core and break down this cranky, worn-out system. I need a spark, something radical enough to shift my mantra from “What am I doing on Earth?” to “Why do not I just enjoy what life is giving?” so that I can justify to myself that I am not wasting my time deceiving myself into the biggest gaslighting incident ever known to mankind. In the meantime, I guess I will try my best to block out these melodramatic, unimaginable scenarios, just to make my life a little more breathable and daring enough for my mental wounds to heal. I will be ready to act a little weirder and bring some thrill into this brain of mine. Joe can turn into a cat, and his mom can let go of her concerns, and they are fictional characters based on human beings, so maybe I am more capable than I think I am. I just need to start believing that more, I guess.
