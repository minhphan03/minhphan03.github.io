I watched "Soul" for the first time a short time after its release, and I didn't remember much of it aside from a disdainful impression that it was very forgettable. I was very wrong. I rewatched it tonight, and I wonder if I was too young to understand the profound meaning behind the movie and that maybe it was even one of the best original feature films Pixar has released. The plot is super original, and the adventure that Joe and 22 embarked on together in the purpose of reviving Joe himself, and at the same time searching for a life purpose for a directionless, witty 22, was magical, brimming with worldly lessons, and emotional. It’s been a while since I got so immersed in a movie for the whole first hour, so to say that it is an achievement to be granted.

Throughout the movie, we saw Joe’s desperation to “come back” or revive himself after falling into the strange land between death and the afterlife. As a result, he missed out on the definitive opportunity to perform with popular jazz musician Dorothea Williams. Still, through that incident, he was able to find out what life was worth. Resurrected as a cat instead of himself, he was forced to take matters into his hands and guide 22 into navigating New York City’s life while discovering himself from the perspective of an outsider. The story of his barber friend, Dez, hinted at this idea. Despite having a dream of being a veterinarian, he was unfortunately forced to give up on it and settle down to be where he was then. Still, he was not disappointed in the slightest. To him, life has more to offer than what he once aspired to do. In other words, a spark does not define someone’s path in life. This was then reiterated in Jerry’s words: “Your spark isn’t your purpose. That last box fills in when you’re ready to come live.” Before landing down on Earth and entering a baby, a new soul has to fill in a “spark,” which, in my own terms, may mean a proper, valid reason to “serve” on Earth. Everyone was born to not be nothing, so in that case, a spark may guide them through, along with their personalities, the first steps of life. However, life is random (and in this movie, only happens once, no recurrence ad infinitum), so one’s lightness should only stem from expecting all the facets of life and the randomness it brings. Joe fell down a manhole was an unpredictable event, yet from that event, he learned so much about himself and about the desire to just “live.”

In a way, this movie answered so much about my current identity crisis at the age of 20. It was not until now that I thought about it for the first time (if you read my previous posts, you can quickly point that fact out). I first proactively chewed over it when I watched "Handsome Devil" (2016). I hit play on that movie expecting it to be for shit and giggles, as a lighthearted movie for the weekend night to just chill and relax. Turned out it was more serious than that. Unbeknownst to the film crew, I found the movie to turn out not quite what they hoped I would… it was not a masterpiece, as much as that “serious” scene led me to believe, and so it was not. I found it to be badly executed in a way that the hackneyed tropes and predictable plot details squashed away any of my excitement. However, there was this one beautiful quote that ignited my interest to evaluate how terrible my identity crisis has been. At the beginning of the movie, Andrew Scott, in the role of a queer English teacher, quoted: “You spend your whole life being someone else; who’s going to be you?”

“Soul” reminded me of this movie somehow. I don’t know why I thought of it when I was watching the other, but I’m always glad to draw some connections between different movies as an expansion of my “cinematic universe.” I think it resonates strongly to my situation during this period of downtime, where I don’t actively participate in college life anymore, and I temporarily lost the only identity I associate myself with: being a college student. Graduation has its prices. I don’t know what I am doing with my life now that I don’t do homework anymore or work part-time at the library I had been working for two years. At least I still do some outside tech projects that at least bring me some sense of value and self-worth. I know I should not fetter myself with these outside influences and instead rummage through my inner self for much-needed stability. In the day and age, it’s not easy to just do something like that. I got overexposed to social media and its anxiety-inducing tactics, and now that I have nothing to do, I cannot help but lean further into it. I wanted so bad to be those people I admired with great jobs, memorable experiences, and exciting traveling trips. I talked to my therapist endless times about the same topic that I grew afraid she would grow sick of reiterating her opinions over and over again, just so that I would retreat back to my old ways as I am too much of a man-child or too pig-headed to attempt any idea of reinvention. I write these words down, but I doubt I would remember them three months later. She told me recently how it cost her almost half of her 20s to figure out what to do with her life, from a BA in English to years of serving at a fine restaurant, which she loved, and a dead-end child services job that kindled her passion with therapy and acted as a final push to her pursuit of a Master's degree in Psychology. She told me that life was not a straight path that you can force to your will, and through bumping with different aspects of it could you maybe find what you love.

This is also the lesson that Joe learned throughout the movie: you don’t need a clear, monumental purpose in life to be a winner in the rat race called life, but simply a passion to live and to experience could be enough as you find your true identity. Conor Masters, the closeted titular character in "Handsome Devil," underwent a self-oppression period before he broke through the misogynistic, patriarchal ideals to truly express whom he was meant to be. “Who’s going to be you?” is not just a rhetorical question alluding to self-identification. It’s an exasperation, a desire to learn and unlearn what is pushed upon oneself. For Joe to unlearn that his only sole pathway to success is to become a jazz artist, and for 22 to unlearn that there is no “spark” on Earth that would entice her to begin her life, we realize that maybe we all need a shake-up, a period of upheaval to disrupt and deconstruct our pigheaded, dogmatic beliefs, those that we pride on too much to relinquish. We all live in our bubbles of absolute convenience and are afraid to take risks, and I know because I am the biggest example of it all. I guess, “to come live” is to take your opportunities to push yourself to the most uncomfortable limits insofar as they are within your limits limits (like, don’t go insane).

For instance, I am, you can say, someone who is always afraid of everything: financial instability, dependence on parents, figuring things on my own, joblessness. I troubled my loved ones enough and tore my pride too much to have the mental capacity to put myself into any more uncomfortable situations, but at the same time, I feel like I am nowhere near there yet. I guess that happened because I need to learn more about this world that I should, and with little time that I have before my graduation, when I will be able to truly start doing what I want to do, I am torn between two pieces of myself: a part of me wants to handle more responsibilities of an adult, and the other part wants to just get it done and over with. It’s easier said than done that your life is yours to experience, because what is implied is that you one hundred percent control your fate and who you want to be, and that’s simply remotely not true in my case. Years of trauma and yearning fettered my willingness to take risks and experience and make way for a grounded way to live, to take on a more reserved and carefully controlled perspective on people around me.

Joe’s story would never turn out to be the way it is if it weren’t for that little adventure, wouldn’t it? Maybe I need something like that, something to shake the foundation to the core and break down this cranky, worn-out system. I need a spark, something radical enough to shift my mantra from “what I am doing on Earth?” to “why don’t I just enjoy what life is giving?” so that I can justify myself that I am not wasting my time deceiving myself into the biggest gaslighting incident ever known to mankind. In the meantime, I guess I’d try my best to block these melodramatic, unimaginable scenarios out of my head, just for my life to be a little bit more breathable and daring enough for my mental wounds to heal, and I am ready to act a little bit weirder and bring some thrill into this brain of mine. Joe can turn into a cat, and his mom can let down her concerns, and they are fictional characters based on human beings, so maybe I am more capable than I think I do. I just need to start believing in that more, I guess.
