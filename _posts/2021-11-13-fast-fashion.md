*A small research paper compiled by Minh Phan for English Composition II course, 2020.*

The era of the booming population has witnessed the rising of new fashion brands in which they focused on simple dressing designs that could be produced quickly and trendy ones that could attract people’s eyes with one single glance. These brands are examples of the newly coined term by the New York Times in the 1990s “fast fashion,” indicating how fast the process of designing to manufacturing to selling that these brands managed to. They are favoured by lots of people for their capability to capture trendy styles, affordability and wide product range. However, it is noticeable that every new trend always comes with benefits and downsides, and this latest advancement in the fashion industry is not the exception. In all, I believe that this trend is doing more harm than good, with the advantages barely cover the deplorable reality behind.

There are many reasons why fast fashion is hyped, and one of its undeniable advantages is how its clothes are comparatively more affordable than non-fast-fashion clothes. According to Solene Rauturier, by optimizing logistics operation and manufacturing, fast fashion brands can cut production and transportation costs to a great extent before the products reach the stores. By sampling ideas and trendy clothing from designer brands’ collections or the celebrity culture, these brands can replicate newest styles and get them on the market as soon as possible without much investment in the designing process. And by using cheaper materials, the perfect combination is complete (Rauturier). The results are clothes that maintain affordability for the significant public to enjoy their shopping trips without hurting their wallets. They are the key to reach the general public, as brands like H&M and Zara pop up their stores on the most crowded streets downtown in many big cities around the world.

However, there is more than just the price tag that makes fast fashion the newest trend of the era. The definition of fast fashion reveals it all. Compared to regular fashion brands, fast fashion brands create around 52 “micro-seasons” per year, which means that one new “season” every week (VanDyke). Therefore, they can replicate trendy clothes at a fast pace and bring the latest trends into their product lines at an even quicker pace. By this way, customers can choose from a wide range of clothes any time they need without worrying that they will go out of style. Also, as each micro-season is transient, brands can reintroduce styles faster than usual so shoppers can visit them at any time in the year and still find something they need. Also, since retailers also sell all-year-round apparels (tees, jeans, dresses), divide sections for genders (men and women) and age (teenagers, adults, children), and offer accessories (purses, belts, hats, etc.), these brands are the go-to for everyday essentials to trendy apparels. Customers can go to these brands retail stores to purchase everything they need with comfort, without the need to shop multiple brands for different clothing types. This is how fast fashion has its name: it allows customers to purchase fashion garments relatively instantly.

The domestic economy of fast fashion brands and manufacturers’ nations also receive economic benefits. For many brands with their headquarters located in developed countries, the growing population is the key to thriving. According to a scientific paper published on Sciendo, the global apparel industry is expected to have a significant growth by about 5.91% and the e-commerce market by 10.36% for the next three years (2022) (Knošková and Garasová). The considerable progress in the industry comes from the frequent shopping activity of people, and the optimistic projection is driven from a growing middle-class population across the globe, which is the main target of these brands. The economic contribution from these fast fashion brands also benefit manufacturers in developing countries. Many U.S. fast fashion brands like H&M and Zara have to outsource the majority of production to countries with cheaper labour (Linden). Some of these countries include Vietnam, China, India and Bangladesh. By outsourcing the apparel manufacturing, the retailers can gain in size and reputation (in these nations) and create jobs for workers in these developing countries and reduce unemployment. According to a report published on American Apparel Manufacturers Association, “Large retailers and manufacturers now control vast networks of global supply chains and with imports accounting for 74% of domestic apparel consumption” (Doeringer and Crean). By focusing on imported goods, the trend leads to the need for logisticians, drivers, managers, etc., resulting in high demand for the workforce.

Despite many foreseeable benefits of fast fashion, there are deadly weak points of this fashion trend that one can quickly point out. The biggest one is the environmental impact. Because of outsourcing, the transportation of products would result in a bigger carbon footprint in the environment through ships and aeroplanes. Furthermore, the cheap fabrics commonly used in production contains large percentages of polyester, a synthetic material derived from petroleum. Hence, it is plastic, which is notoriously destructive to the habitat. Most of the polyester fabrics used in fast fashion apparels are non-biodegradable (Hodakel). Although helping to lower production costs, cotton-polyester materials are disposable and hence harmful for the environment once trashed into the landfill. The apparel production, likewise, is destructive. According to an article published on U.N. Environment, the fashion industry accounts for 20% of wastewater and 10% of carbon emission worldwide every year. For a pair of jeans, an average of 2000 gallons of water is used, and for a T-shirt, and a garbage truck of textiles goes into the environment every second (“Putting”). The amount of water needed to make a cotton shirt is enough to serve water for a person in 2.5 years, and the carbon emission for a jean’s production equals that of a car running around 80 miles (Drew and Reichart). On the other hand, the use of electricity in these factories also leads to air and sound pollution. Hence, one can deduce that the (fast) fashion industry at the moment is unsustainable to the Earth, as it wastes lots of resources and produces harmful chemicals to our environment without any indication that the trend will stop anytime soon.

The ethical side of the industry is also problematic. According to information from the International Labor Organization (ILO) cited in a The Guardian’s report, around 11% of the world’s children are labour exploited, with any of them working in the fashion supply chain producing textiles and garments to supply for the apparel industry (Moulds). Also, unfiltered wastewater and effluent replete with chemicals and microfibers can cause health problems like cancer, body deformation and poisoning for residents around factories (Chua). On the other hand, the low wage, paid by the fast fashion brands to maximize profit and lower production costs as possible, of workers, is a big question mark on the sustainability of the trend. According to a report on the clothing consumption and sustainability, most of women and girls workers in the garment industry works “up to six or seven days a week,” with some are “burnt out and physically unable to continue beyond their 30s” (Environmental Audit Committee). In that same report, the authors cited a supply chain consultant quote, in which she feared that a 13.3% decline in paid money to suppliers in Bangladesh is concerning, especially when prices are supposed to go up due to inflation. The unrealistic price offers on apparel manufacturers ultimately acts on workers, whose minimum wage has already too low to improve their living conditions. With that minimum payment, they still have to deliver maximum productivity, as the global population increases and leads to even more demand in garments and apparels, and work excessive hours to support themselves.

The wave of fast fashion brands does not only affect on a global scale; it also impacts on a personal scale – particularly, human’s psychological state. Neurologically speaking, shopping is a complex process, and according to research in 2007, there is a “specific patterns of brain activation predict purchasing” (Brian et al.). They found that as the study subjects’ saw their desirable objects on sale, the pleasure centre in the brain (nucleus ambens) lit up, indicating that the exhilaration in shopping comes from the psychological phenomenon of pursuing and waiting for goods. This reaction kicks in also merely from the act of looking or dealing with potential gain and loss. (Kuhnen and Knutson). Fast fashion brands are masters in exploiting this neurological process and turn it into marketing tactics. Brands like H&M or Zara offer frequent promotions because they know that customers’ hedonic pleasure centres in the brain light up every time they receive promotional emails in their inbox. They also enable customers to buy (reaped) high-end designer apparels with a small fraction of the cost. The key lies in the “inexpensive” good deal that makes one think one’s shopping clothes are bargains, bringing pleasures and stimulating one’s desire to continue shopping. With all of these tactics, customers became prisoners in an endless willingness to spend money on excessive apparels, which in return boosting sales and compensating for the brands’ low prices (Bain and Quartz). They wanted to turn shopping into an entertainment form that one cannot resist and frequently engage in it, and with the way that it works right now, they can’t be more successful.

Fast fashion emerged when Zara was established in 1975 and became the pillar of the fashion industry ever since, but the future of this business model is still a question mark. On one side, many cannot deny its potentials to the world: it levels the shopping experience up to a standard entertainment form, and businesses benefit on it on a grand scale. The diversity of styles and types in brands allows customers to wander around shopping malls without running out of excitement. However, the weak side of this is that it makes shopping an uncontrollable addiction that not many people take serious attention to. The fast fashion industry also benefits the economy and create jobs, which, on a positive note, is a good sign considering the world population is continuously on the rise. However, those jobs are atrocious to workers in developing countries, as abject working conditions and living wages fail to provide security. The strong point of fast fashion, once again, comes from its affordability. By making products more affordable, brands can reach out to a broader range of customers in the middle class and enable them to shop for clothes economically. However, the deadliest point of the trend comes from the aftermath of its affordability: environmental issues such as disposable, unrecyclable garments damaging habitats; moral damage involved; and bad spending habits. All things considered, I’m strongly convinced to the unsustainable side of fast fashion because for all the affordability and convenience of fast fashion we have to pay an insurmountable price for the environment and millions of lives around the world. Its consequences, namely health deterioration and nature contamination, would take decades and generations to deal with, and the most important thing is, I think we are not ready to face them yet.
