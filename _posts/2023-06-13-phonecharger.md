Some people will say that this is nothing groundbreaking or worthy of writing an entry about, but for someone who suffers greatly from social anxiety, it is a significant achievement (and a much-needed action) for me. I am fully aware of how isolated I have tried to make myself appear, and it takes a toll on my confidence to ask someone for something out of fear of rejection.

Three weeks ago, I was on my way to the train station in Seattle to go to Portland when I realized that I had left my power bank at home. To make matters worse, my phone battery was about to die, and I desperately needed it to look up directions around the city as I had never been there before. When I arrived at the station, I was one of the last people to board the train, so I didn’t have any time to ask the people at the station counter if they had a charger. My plan was to rush to the Apple Store in Portland and ask for free charging while I purchased a new iPhone for my mom. However, I needed to download the city map offline, and in the process of doing that, my battery level dropped so low that I feared it might not finish. There was a girl sitting across from me who was charging her AirPods case, and after ten minutes of contemplating various scenarios on how I should approach her, I finally decided to take a shot. I leaned across the walkway and asked if I could use the charger. Unfortunately, she immediately rejected my request, saying that she was using it, and I was left stunned. I hadn’t anticipated that she wouldn’t offer an explanation or offer to lend it to me at a later time. I should have offered those solutions myself. If I had, she might not have outright refused. Regardless, the whole exchange lasted only five minutes. I retreated back to my seat, berating myself for being cowardly, and became even more determined to go straight to the Apple Store after reaching Portland.

But the story didn’t end there. There was a guy in front of the girl who was also charging his phone. In front of him, there was a couple both working on their laptops. The husband asked the guy if he could borrow his socket because their chargers couldn’t fit into their charging outlet simultaneously. He agreed, and it turned out that his charger also blocked his outlet. As he went to put his charger away, I spontaneously reached out and asked if I could have it. I was still “traumatized” by the earlier “disaster,” but you know how sometimes, when you’re desperate, you let go of rational thoughts and rely on natural instinct: survival mode. I saw a golden opportunity for my sanity and “survival,” and I wasted no time thinking about what could have been. The idea of Apple Store chargers seemed enticing when I wasn’t fully focused on redeeming myself from the earlier embarrassment, but I was indeed focused, and I acted before my social anxiety demons could convince me that I shouldn’t appear like a beggar in front of others and ask for more pity and shame.

So I asked him, and he reluctantly gave in. Still processing the information, I quickly said thanks, grabbed the charger, and gracefully returned to my seat. I spent the next 30 minutes charging my phone, feeling grateful for the sudden bravery that emerged, and replayed the encounter in my head. It felt surreal back then, and probably still does now because it’s not something I would typically do. I mean, I would gladly strike up a conversation with a stranger when I’m not in a vulnerable position, but in that situation, all I could think about was how I might invade somebody’s space and disrupt their journey. It happened the first time. It materialized my biggest fear: how frightening it is to be pushed into a dilemma where you have to reach out and ask someone for something, expecting them to refuse, in the hopes of escaping your own misery or be consumed by it.

I returned the charger to the man around ten minutes before the train arrived in Portland. I still went to the Apple Store to charge my phone, but I didn’t have to stay as long as I would have otherwise, and I was able to navigate my way around the city for the day. To be honest, downtown was underwhelming, but traveling alone can sometimes be liberating. My phone battery died again on the train back to Seattle, but I knew the city’s transit system like the back of my hand, so I didn’t feel the need to ask for a charger again. The mental exercise I had to go through just for something as simple as that was enough, especially since the closest passenger behind me didn’t have an iPhone charger either.

Moral of the story: DON’T FORGET YOUR CHARGER.
