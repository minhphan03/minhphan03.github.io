A final course essay I wrote for HSTAS 265: The Viet Nam Wars, taught by Professor Christoph Giebel at University of Washington - Seattle. Words are unedited before posting.

---

The Vietnam War is never an easy topic to talk about. It is ambiguous, heartbreaking, deadly, and full of stories to tell. As these stories are read and analyzed, we come to a more worldly perspective on those whose blood has bled, and tears have fallen in this havoc. It formally ended almost fifty years ago, but the impact the war left behind is still too loud to speak into words, let alone for those who were directly entangled in it. After reading Dang Thuy Tram's diary and Lam Quang Thi and Le Ly Hayslip's books, I have gained immeasurable insight into a period in the history of my home country in such a humane way beyond the historical numbers and events schools have ever taught. The variance in origins, beliefs, and culture of these authors have given them their unique perspectives and experiences to paint together a powerful political picture of the war in the country in the 20th century. Notably, their upbringings play a significant role in which side they identify as a part of. 

Their social status played a big role in their identity. In her book, Le Ly Hayslip described her upbringing in the countryside of Vietnam as one of many children of two peasants working on her family's farm. She was just a little girl back then, living up to the expectations of a child: helping on the farm, taking after her siblings, and working on house chores (Hayslip & Wurts, 2017). Dang Thuy Tram's background as a daughter of two doctors living in Hanoi allowed her to live a pleasant and quiet childhood: "For a long time I forgot the feelings of a schoolgirl from Chu Van An sitting there with the tip of her pen in her mouth listening to the teacher but not paying attention, just looking at West Lake obscured by the drizzle....thinking of something else" (Dang, 2007). She then went to nursing school in Hanoi, taking after her parents’ footsteps. Thi's background was more bourgeois as his father inherited wealth from a wealthy family in Bac Lieu province, and his mother shared ties with influential people in the Southern region at the time (Lam, 2001). He went to College Phan Thanh Gian with its "complex of well-designed two-story brick buildings." One can clearly see that the latter two were more privileged and hence more likely to be politically influenced by the state of affairs at the time. How could Hayslip worry herself over these outlandish concepts when her family’s peasantry life already left her with limited education and struggle of survival in a new war taking shape? Yet, the arrival of the revolutionary and local forces in the her villaged forced her to learn it the hard way. They imprisoned the young girl, raped, interrogated, and threatened to kill her over baseless allegations. Now that her innocence was lost in the worst way imaginable, as a result, she took on herself a mission to "stand up" against both sides to protect what was left of her life and future: "Both sides in this terrible, endless, stupid war had finally found the perfect enemy: a terrified peasant girl who would endlessly and stupidly consent to be their victim." She then had to flee her hometown and village, where many traditional Vietnamese considered their roots. Before her departure to Danang, her mother reasoned: "The village has turned against you. You can forget about living a normal life." To them, a "normal life" only exists in their villages and family. Once she left that village, it was "karma" and "punishment," and only "[going to your ancestors] is the best any of [them] can hope for these days." If nothing was worth living for and death became a hope, one must assimilate how dire and distressing these villagers must have endured. 

Moving to Danang and Saigon, she witnessed how the war impacted the people she loved and society at the time. Constant death news and division of her loved ones bombarded the young girl as she took on the responsibilities of a mother. She talked about slave traffic of young and naïve Vietnamese girls like her, some of whom were not even in puberty yet, coming into the city only to be exploited and sexually abused without knowing they were selling themselves. She witnessed how these women faced sexual racism and abuse by American GIs under the pretense of "payback" for their help in the country. She knew that they used her, but at the same time, she realized that they did not take the matter of "independence" as seriously as Vietnamese fighters did. She reluctantly found a sense of inner peace in these strangers, away from the perpetual question of the meaning of the war. She took the middle ground in the debate, shielding herself and believing that there is no right side everyone should take. 

Nevertheless, not everyone chose to stay neutral. Unlike Hayslip, Dang Thuy Tram and Lam Quang Thi were voluntarily involved in the Vietnam War for the opposing sides, thanks to how their upbringings had shaped their beliefs. The former dedicated the most beautiful years of her life under the bombings and shootings as a battlefield surgeon in Quang Ngai Province. Growing up in a middle-class family in Hanoi under communist influence, she felt ashamed for being a "petty bourgeois." She felt compelled to move south and contribute to the country's reunification because to be a true communist, one has to sacrifice themselves for the greater good. She has seen youngsters whose futures were ruined by the war decided to stand up against their colonizers. Stories of "Hoang, fourteen years old, has killed five American soldiers in the first six months" and "An Pho Chau has captured five guns," written in one of Dang's entries, proved the point. These people did not have a choice: they merely sided with the opponent of their disruptors out of revenge. As she left her family behind in Hanoi and ventured down South of Vietnam into the battlefields, she was appalled by the sense of fragility and triviality that the war had wrecked upon these people, as her patients piled up and brothers and sisters laid down their lives. 

She poured all her whims into the pages filled with death, pain, sweat, blood, and tears. "Sadness and sorrow cannot be shown with tears now" or "why in these few days has my mind been so heavy with thoughts?" showcase the young comrade's most vulnerable moments in her life. She blamed herself for being soft while her beloved ones were suffering: "I feel that I am to blame...in the fire of the country why is my heart naturally weak?" She was dire with hope and expressed her yearning for her family, and especially her "adopted" brothers and sisters: "[I come to the classes] with also the love of a big sister for those young brothers and sisters who have to bear all of the suffering and losses..." As the Americans destroyed their native land, she believed that the only way to save her country was to build a socialist country, the one thing that their nemesis have considered their greatest threat: "...we have to fully concentrate on fighting the Americans... focus our strength on building a socialist people, the perfect society which knows how to live for the people, how to live a life of culture." To her, socialism was a solution, an escape from war. Only building a socialist society would bring people happiness. This is essentially the propaganda that the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (DRVN) used to influence the population at the time to fight against the U.S. and the Republic of Vietnam (RVN). Dang was enraptured by its appeal as there was no such thing as an alternative, but would she continue to pursue that ideology if she outlived the war? That is one thing we will never know, but one thing is for sure: she was an absolute believer in the Party, and politics motivated her to stay in Quang Ngai during the darkest days of her life. 

Unlike the female authors, Lam did not go into much detail about the human cost of the war. As a general officer, his concerns revolved around military tactics, the war's progress, and Saigon's political stream as he tried to build up his career. He saw the first glimpses of the war as coup d’état’s and political rearrangements around the 1940s took place in Bac Lieu, particularly when "American fighter planes strafing a wedding procession on the Co Co River with machine guns." Lam expressed contempt for the revolutionary forces as they threatened his family's affairs and social standing. By directly getting involved in the military, he was able to maintain it and boosted his career to unprecedented heights. The young general’s book, therefore, was swamped with political and military affairs, and rightfully so: the militarization of RVN and American intervention impacted almost every aspect of the society at the time. He believed the two Republic governments messed up and corruption ruined their opportunities. He retold his (and others') battles across the country. He expressed nostalgia, regret, and a glimmer of hope at the end of the war ("We didn't know we had a choice"). What came out at the end was a sense of pride in the bygone prime, a fallen sovereign that no matter how much he boasted about his victories, military tactics, and personal achievements, what remained was only reminiscence. He thrived by the war and it reflected in his drastically biased viewpoint all along. To him, it was a game of minds and power. Everything boiled down to tactics, numbers, and doing anything to defeat the enemies, like a chess game. A pure recount of what happened, a documentary of his glory days. He expressed limited failures or negative feelings, unless it was for decorum (of a memoir) "I was saddened by the fall of Danang," or "I was saddened by the news that Col. Nguyen Van Thanh... was killed with his troops." A simple "emotion-driven" sentence followed by another pool of events detailing the war's timeline. One can be confident to say he would not bat an eye at how Hayslip and most of the region's population barely survived through the day, or be sympathetic to Dang and her tragic tales of the brutality that bombs and guns his forces have left behind. Nevertheless, could anybody blame him? After all, he was performing his rightful duties as a military officer, and victories are more than desirable. He did nothing wrong by vocalizing his thoughts or taking pride on his successful military career (until it wasn’t), but at the same time he was a war criminal, one to have had blood of innocent people like Dang and Hayslip on his hands. 

The authors' upbringing certainly influenced their politics, but ultimately, the question is whether it was worth taking sides. No matter what background and political stance the authors took after, they all ended up losing a piece of themselves: Dang sacrificing in the foreign land over the call for national independence, Thi losing his homeland and career, and Hayslip unwillingly severing ties with her family members. Looking at the political state of affairs on a larger scale, the Vietnam War was nothing but a human slaughterhouse for the big powers. Their opposition drove innocent Vietnamese people into misery as they became pawns and numbers in a game they never asked to be a part of. Dang, Lam, and Hayslip's distinctive backgrounds from each other may have defined their politics and biased views towards other parties, but by reading between the lines one could see how they were one and the same: they were all victims of the war whether they made it out or not. Adorning their narratives with lavishing, lighthearted, and juicy details may bring sympathy, but the trauma is still there for them and millions of other Vietnamese. No alternative in background, narrative, and politics can heal the pain they have carried for years and years.
