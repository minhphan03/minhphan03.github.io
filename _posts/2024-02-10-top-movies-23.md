I began watching movies more frequently at the starting of last year, thanks to Letterboxd. I felt like I was missing out on a culture so grandiose ever since I found out how many movies I have had the opportunity to watch but ended up ignoring their existences. So, I began this journey of discovering myself through movies, learning about the human development and all their beautiful ideas through cinema. I did not end up liking every movie I have watched, but for those I did, I was grateful by the decision I have made. I had more meaningful conversations with people talking about movies, recognized cultural references I have never heard before, and discovered a cinema culture/community full of like-minded people. 2023 would not end properly without a list of my favorite, because I spent hundreds of hours watching movies in my room, fighting against boredom and short attention span. I promised this in this year's [annual post on my birthday](https://minhphan03.com/2023/12/06/twenty). Again, just like my favorite music blog post, this list is of course subjective. 

<h3>January: The Silence of the Lambs (1991) </h3>

I watched this movie with a friend of mine on the very first trip to New York (and he cut contact with me after that, who knows the reason why). It was truly a masterpiece, I don’t know. Hannibal’s eyes truly deserved an Oscar given how terrifying they were. Those eyes were truly horror, concretized. In hindsight it gave me a very first thrill into watching movies as a hobby, or as a means of entertainment. I say it again and again: *The Silence of the Lambs* is truly one of the movies for the ages.

<h3>February: Notting Hill (1999)</h3>

Before *Notting Hill*, my favorite romcom was *The Proposal* (2009). I had gone through a three month crush on young Ryan Reynolds over that movie, and God truly knows how obsessed I was with Sandra Bullock — she was MOTHER embodied (if the definition of the world existed back then), but then Julia Roberts and Hugh Grant had to arrive and knocked me on my ass with their tender, cozy chemistry in this movie. Oh how many times I had wished to live in Notting Hill, barely scraping by being a travel bookstore owner and having my famous lover sweeping me off my feet so bad!

<h3>March: Three Colors: Blue (1993)</h3>

I watched the *Three Colors* trilogy in two days and had the hardest time choosing between the Blue or Red establishment to be my favorite movie of the month. I finally settled down on *Blue*, the first installment, just because it had a clearer storytelling style and plot that redefined what the color blue is: freedom. In *Blue*, Julie found freedom in the most unexpected situation: her husband and daughter’s deaths. Grief and shame overwhelmed the poor woman as she felt in love with her husband’s collaborator Olivier whose music swayed and rejuvenated her back to life.

<h3>April: Apocalypse Now (1979)</h3>

I have to admit that the sole reason I decided to put this movie in my watchlist is because it is inspired by Vietnam War. Turns out it was not only about the Vietnam War, which made me really glad. As much as I wanted people to know about my country, I hate them only know about the war whenever they mention it. *Apocalypse Now* also based loosely on the book *Heart of Darkness*, a novella about a captain gone rouge on native lands. While the first half did not leave anything unsatisfied, the latter half was frantic, gory, horrifying, and epic. It was so dark, so real, and so terrifying watching how the twisted the human nature can be due to the extreme circumstances they are in. Francis Ford Coppola deserved all the flowers. He truly did for every single one of them.

<h3>June: Memento (2000)</h3>

*Memento* was truly one of the ages. The somewhat roundabout, circling reverse-chronological plot timeline, instead of chasing me away, intrigued me to no end. His own homicide and Leonard’s inability to store recent memories forced him to rely on written documents, polaroids, and tattoos, which at the same time opened up an exciting perspective on the characters. The colored frames of a somewhat frantic Leonard on his mission to find John G dichotomize the black and white frames of a somber Leonard just sitting on a motel room bed with a phone on his hand granted me a deeper study into a struggling character, both with his surroundings (being used) and himself (finding a meaning of his life — taking revenge on the men whom assaulted him and his wife). Truly a mindfuck movie, but a good movie nonetheless. Christopher Nolan utilized black and white so well in here, giving me Kill Bill vibes, which was also one of my favorite movie series.

<h3>July: 12 Angry Men (1957)</h3>

People were not wrong when they rated this movie so high on Letterboxd: it truly standed the test of time, and for good and not so good reasons. Great for how inspiring it is, and bad for how relevant it is. A simple setting of twelve men in a meeting room debating whether they would convict a marginalized young man of homicide turned out to have so many lessons and interesting details to talk about. To witness the witty, concrete reasonings from the jurors and how each and every one of them switched their votes from “guilty” to “innocent” made me smile and happy to no ends. In an era where racism was very much present in life (white actors dominating the movie industry), it’s refreshing that it is condemned harshly by calm, sound, and nonpartisan men whom very much deserved to be where they were.

<h3>August: To Kill a Mockingbird (1962)</h3>

I read on the news recently that many students hate to read *To Kill a Mockingbird* and in response to that, many teachers were also removing them from their lessons. It made me sad, because it was one of the very few English books I enjoyed. The movie adaptation also became one of my favorites, with Gregory Peck’s stellar acting as Atticus Finch playing a big role in my enjoyment. Like *12 Angry Men*, *To Kill A Mockingbird* played with the topic of race, alongside more exotic topics such as lost of innocence and fatherhood. In black and white, the movie directed my focus on the storytelling and mystery around Maycomb, Alabama, where racism was very much a rampant issue and justice was rarely served. Atticus, knowing beforehand that his efforts would go futile, still went to great lengths to support and save Tom Robinson from false accusations. It was an emotional ride, in which I would never forget it.

<h3>September: Spotlight (2015)</h3>

In many ways, *Spotlight* could have gone wrong like *Bombshell* (2019) did. Both drama-documentaries, trying to expose entities that could shake the state of affairs, and I’m so glad it didn’t. Not that the latter’s subject was not as cool or jarring as the former, but it seems half-baked and completely misconstrued the actual story. Why it was, I’m not going to explain in-depth because this is not a review for it (read the Guardian’s article explaining it [here](https://amp.theguardian.com/film/2020/jan/18/bombshell-review-charlize-theron-nicole-kidman-margot-robbie)). *Spotlight*, on the other hand, zooms in the collective effort and went in deep to expose the inhumane sex trafficking network of pedophilic pastors and the collateral damage these people inflicted upon their victims. Heartbreaking, alarming, yet thrilling, I appreciate and enjoy the courage, determination, and sharp intellect that the reporters and journalists of the Spotlight team within the Boston Globe has exerted through the film to shed the light on this horrific sex abuse system that was masked and protected by individuals whom are supposed to guide and protect their civilians. The film pace is quick, intense, and slow at the right time, enough to keep my interest at bay, and boy was it a luck that documentaries would not make my eyes roll like hell, because believe me documentaries are not my thing. Which makes this one such an excellent piece of cinema. Not going to watch it again, though.

<h3>October: Taylor Swift: The Eras Tour (2023)</h3>

At the point of writing this, I have already watched the concert movie in theaters for four times already. It is THAT captivating. I loved the moments of Swifities dancing and doing jokes together, especially the later two shows where I watched in Vietnam. It was kind of a rare moment to witness how everyone was truly having a good time together and their high-spirited fan love channeling into the chants, dances, and singalongs. Taylor’s music has been on my rotation for years and years since I was a primary school student in fourth grade, and looking at her current reign in the music industry (and now the movie industry — *Taylor Swift: The Eras Tour* broke numerous records as a concert film) was such a sweet, satisfying moment to behold. I knew she would make it big back then, and I still believe she does and will have new tricks under her sleeve now, as a seasoned performer and touring act. Her effortless charm and star power was truly unmatched in the movie. Nobody would see this concert to becoming back then, and I’m so glad to say I will go to a fifth and final showing on the 17th of December!

<h3>November: Gaslight (1944)</h3>

*Gaslight*’s best element must be its comparably simple plot: everything takes place mainly in a house and through the mind of a woman. Like *12 Angry Men*, what led the story was the conversations and spectacular, spellbound acting from both Ingrid Bergman and Charles Boyer, whose roles had somewhat popularized the term gaslighting that became a trendy expression nowadays. Bergman’s acting was top-notch at the climax of the movie where she thought she was again being demented despite under deception; I really thought she was giving him a taste of his medicine, which she did at the end (go ma’am). Truly a masterclass of gaslighting (and manipulation, which gaslighting is a part of). I got chills.

<h3>December: City of God (2002)</h3>

*City of God* was a masterclass in the crime/thriller genre, not because of the special effects or how real the fighting scenes and bloodshed crimes are, but because of the social annotations and revelations that the plot developed through all the slum gangs and their mentality to do anything to survive in a hostile, corrupted, and gruesome society that all but wipe clean their destine slates. Witnessing his loved ones tethered to shreds through street ruling breakouts, cops whose guns have claimed innocent lives, and drug addiction, the narrator (Buscapé) unveiled so vividly a community where power and dominance is key to survive, and death is a normal part of life. Benny’s death out of protecting a good friend of his, or Lil Z’s sovereign over the area resulted in his perish over the natural cycle of generational gang formation, these events demystify and push the boundaries of the great lengths that kids are influenced through the environment where they were brought up. Robberies, laundering, and addiction pulled these people in and trapped them in an endless cycle of crime one might lose their life trying to get around. It’s a grim life for the poor and the innocence to outgrow the trauma that they had no clue would come and claim their sanity.
