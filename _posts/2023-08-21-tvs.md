*SPOILER ALERT AHEAD!*

It happens to me, like 80% of the time, that a movie I regard as a masterpiece is an adaptation of a book. This is the case for “The Virgin Suicides,” a 1999 drama-romance film directed by Sofia Coppola based on the original book published in 1993 by Jeffrey Eugenides. I did not hold much expectation for the movie before watching it, given my prior experience watching “Lost in Translation” last month, also directed by Coppola, and ended up not liking it at all. However, this movie moved me, so I guess it’s more about the storyline than the direction that impacts how I review a movie. For this thinkpiece, I want to go deeper into the message behind “The Virgin Suicides” three days after watching it rather than the plot as I always forget like 99% of it after watching anything.

In my opinion, the story follows the themes of overprotection, public image, mental health, and first loves. I thought it would be a normal boring coming-of-age drama about love until the youngest child in the family of five daughters, Cecilia, committed suicide, and the gossip around the case blowed up to the national television. The remaining sisters went to school after the case died down a couple of days later pretending that nothing happened, but almost everyone felt the staggering change in the air. The group of boys who were curious about the case (where the narrative were from) decided to “investigate” the case, partly because they were obsessive over the beautiful girls (typical straight teenage boys syndrome) and also were taken aback by their attitudes. This is where the theme of overprotectiveness comes in: their parents wanted to shield them away from the trauma that the suicide caused. We can see how they tried to behave normally, but there was a scene where one of the boys attemped a conversation with Lux Lisbon and was shocked looking into her lifeless, faraway gaze, even more so after her retort at his embarrassing excuse since they never talked before. She knew the boys were curious, and she wanted to do anything to let go of her heavy heartstrings but held back because of her family’s public image. It was precipitated when the girls’ mother attempted a “classy” Christian family party inviting all the boys to their house to make sure that the neighbors would not suspect why her family was so distant. One could see how Cecilia behaved so different from her sisters joking with the boys right before her suicide attempt, and it was alarming: the mother was so forced trying to contain herself together after she retreated back to her room. She was displeased of her daughter’s behavior, but more so about what the boys would tell their parents about the party and all the rumors would spread like wildfires again. After the suicide, we catch a glimpse of her crying in the bedroom when the Father visited the house and gave up the conversation with the father when he tried to keep himself together and focused on the baseball game on the TV. They all mentally suffered from it. They were barely at the seams keeping everything intact, acting as if their youngest member’s death was just a fleeting event in their grand scheme of life.

But that was not the end of everything. Trip Fontaine, a senior boy at the school, grew interested in Lex and asked the boys to help him invite her to the school prom by escorting the remaining three girls. Both conservative parents, they allowed them to go as long as they went back home before the curfew. Of course, this Cinderella-style trope ended exactly like how it was meant to be: Lex and Trip stayed past the curfew to make out and had sex (in public on a football field, no less) as a rare glimpse into the normal teenage dream she always wanted, angering the parents to the extreme that they decided to pull their kids out of school, confining them to the walls of their house indefinitely. They loved their children, of course, but in a way that was too extreme for anyone to accept it. They showed support of their children when they stood up against the street workers tearing down their front lawn tree, and protected them from the tidbits and malicious gossip out there, but were they too clueless! Exasperated, Lex turned rebellious and started to smoke behind their backs, and her sisters followed suit, deriving ways to communicate with the outside world. The boys caught their clues and sketched out a plan to rescue them for a getaway, but before they could do that and satisfied their obsession with the mysterious figures, the girls took turns to follow Cecilia and fell down one by one, just like a horror movie script destined to haunt all the living beings breathing in the neighborhood (or the local newspapers). Unlike horror movies that highlighted grand horrifying scenes of slaughtering, theirs were silent. They knew the parents could not take another shot of public attention, and they delivered just that. The boys, then growing older and wiser, still seemed to get stuck in the events traumatizing their pubertal years. It unsettled their sanity that wrapped around the guillable girl fascination. Who were they actually? Why did it happen? Was it worth it? Blonde girls, who are often sexualized and shallowed in the media, were then portraited more layered, complex, and puzzling more than ever. Nobody knew who they were and why they did it. Everybody gossiped. Endless tirades blown up to the parents’ faults and accusations of their indirect homicide to the gorgeous, dreamy coming-of-age women. Of their brutal decision to retreat the girls from school, suffered them with claustrophobia and insane level of overprotection. Their public image was then completely tarnished, submerging them in shame and gossip.

After watching the movie, I asked myself so many what-ifs. What if Trip Fontaine was never interested in Lux in the first place? What if Cecilia Lisbon was never the odd one out and mentally able enough to have never committed suicide? The story could have been very different if it were not for her, but like a butterfly effect the tragedy of the Lisbon family was inevitable once the parents crossed the line and triggered the impending bombshell none of them expected to happen. 

Were the parents responsible for everything to turn out in this way? I have mixed opinions. They all but imprisoned the children by pulling them out of school, I agree, but again we still don’t know what happened among them. We barely see what was happening inside the family and what, if happened, terrifying events that occurred behind closed doors. People theorized that Cecilia was suicidal all along regardless of how her parents treated her, but from what the book (and the movie) told us, there seems to be more than just a simple explanation for the domino chain that wrecked havoc onto a classic Americana family.
